Large language models (LLMs) show promising diagnostic and triage performance, yet direct comparisons with healthcare professionals (HCPs) and collaborative effects remain limited. We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of studies (January 2020 to September 2025) comparing the diagnostic or triage accuracy of LLMs, HCPs, or their collaboration across seven databases. Studies using multiple-choice formats rather than open diagnostic generation were excluded. We extracted top-1, top-3, top-5, and top-10 diagnostic and triage accuracies and pooled results using multilevel random-effects models to account for nested observations. Of 10,398 studies screened, 50 met criteria, evaluating 25 different LLMs across diverse medical specialties. The relative diagnostic accuracy of LLMs versus HCPs progressively improved from 0.89 (95% CI, 0.79–1.00) for top-1 to 0.91 (0.83–1.00) for top-3, 1.04 (0.89–1.22) for top-5, and 1.17 (0.87–1.57) for top-10 diagnoses, with significant model variability. LLM-assisted HCPs outperformed HCPs alone, with relative diagnostic accuracy of 1.13 (1.00–1.27) for top-1, 1.11 (1.01–1.23) for top-3, 1.42 (1.16–1.73) for top-5, and 1.33 (0.94–1.87) for top-10 diagnoses. Triage accuracy was similar between LLMs and HCPs (1.01 [0.94–1.09]). These findings show potential for LLM integration but methodological flaws in studies necessitate rigorous real-world evaluation before clinical implementation.

The adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) in healthcare is on the rise, with over 1,000 AI-enabled devices authorized by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)1. This expansion has been further catalyzed by the emergence of large language models (LLMs), which demonstrate remark￾able capabilities without requiring traditional labeled datasets and can tackle tasks beyond their explicit training scope2. Diagnosis and patient triage represent fundamental competencies in healthcare delivery, yet significant challenges persist. Primary care settings experience concerning mis￾diagnosis rates of 20%, contributing to nearly 17% of all adverse events3. Similarly, triage errors, ranging from 2% and 31%, further complicate the clinical landscape, leading to harmful treatment delays and inappropriate resource allocation4. LLMs offer new opportunities to address these challenges5,6, but require comprehensive evaluation and rigorous scrutiny before integration into clinical workflow.

Despite numerous studies showing encouraging LLM performance on medical licensing examinations7–9, these controlled testing environments inadequately reflect the complexity and nuance of clinical settings. Fur￾thermore, most studies lack direct comparisons with healthcare profes￾sionals (HCPs), limiting the contextual relevance of LLM performance metrics. The only available meta-analysis comparing LLM diagnostic accuracy with HCPs reported 52.1% accuracy for LLMs, 9.9% lower than HCPs10. However, this analysis was limited by its inclusion of only 17 pre￾June 2024 studies, an unclear definition of diagnostic accuracy, mixed testing formats that included both multiple-choice questions and free-text responses, and a failure to account for case-specific influences. Additionally, it overlooked triage performance, an important consideration as 13.9% of laypeople now use LLMs as their first-line health consultation tool, with this trend projected to increase11. Importantly, LLMs should augment rather than replace HCPs, yet no systematic review has evaluated the performance of HCPs with and without LLM assistance. Our systematic review addresses these knowledge gaps by providing an updated assessment of LLM performance in disease diagnosis and triage through open dialog compared with HCPs, while also exploring their collaborative potential. We critically appraise study quality, con￾sidering study design, reporting, and clinical value to identify methodolo￾gical limitations in current research. This analysis aims to provide insights into both the risks and potential pathways for responsible clinical integra￾tion of LLMs, with implications for healthcare policy and future research directions in LLM-augmented healthcare delivery.

## Results

### Study selection

Figure 1 illustrates the study selection process. Initially, 10,398 studies were identified through electronic databases and manual searches. After removing duplicates, 4928 studies underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 108full-text articleswere assessedfor eligibility, and 58 were excluded, resulting in 50 studies being included in the meta-analysis2,12–60.

### Study characteristics

The characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1. Among 50 included studies, 17 (34%) were published in 2025, 26 (52%) in 2024, and the remainder in 2023. The United States (n = 12, 24%), China (n = 8, 16%), and Japan (n = 8, 16%) contributed the most studies. Most studies (n = 36, 72%) focused on diagnosis, 9 (18%) on triage, and 5 (10%) on both. Regarding medical specialties, 12 studies (24%) spanned multiple disciplines, followed by emergency medicine (n = 10, 20%), ophthalmology (n = 10, 20.0%), and radiology (n = 8, 16%).

Study design and data collection methods varied. Only 3 studies (6%) used prospective clinical cases, all assessing triage performance. Sixteen studies (32%) employed synthetic patient vignettes, 16 (32%) utilized published case reports, and 16 (32%) retrospectively collected clinical cases from databases. The number of cases ranged from 11 to 6,657. Only 28 studies (56%) reported eligibility criteria, and 15 (30%) provided data on mean age or female proportion. Most studies (88%) included examination results in their case description, while 4 (8%) reported only chief complaints. Most cases were text-based, with 8 (16%) also including images. The majority of studies inputted English text into the LLMs, with 6 (12%) using Chinese. Regarding case disclosure, 24 studies (48%) publicly shared cases, 21 did not (42%), and 5 (10%) partially shared them. The reference standards varied, including expert consensus, recorded answer, discharge summaries, histology, and follow-up outcomes.

A total of 25 different LLMs were evaluated. GPT series were the most frequently investigated, comprising GPT-4 (28 studies), GPT-3.5 (20), GPT-4o (5), GPT-4V (4), and GPT-4o mini and GPT-3 (1 each). Other models included the Gemini series (Gemini, Gemini 1.5 pro, Gemini 1.5 Flash, Gemini 1.0 pro, Gemini Pro Vision), the Claude series (Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Claude 2.1, Claude 3 Opus), DeepSeek series (DeepSeek R1), Google Bard, Bing Chat, Doubao, Grok3, and six medical-domain LLMs (MOPH, MedAIpaca, MedFound, PhenoBrain, DeepSeek Medical, AIME). ### Diagnostic and triage accuracy: LLM versus HCP

Overall accuracy comparison. As shown in Fig. 2, the relative top-1 diagnostic accuracy of LLMs compared to HCPs was 0.89 (95% CI, 0.79–1.00; I2 = 98.1%). The performance gap narrowed for top-3 diag￾nostic accuracy, with a relative accuracy of 0.91 (0.83–1.00; I2 = 98.1%). No significant differences were found between LLMs and HCPs for top-5 (1.04 [0.89–1.22]; I2 = 96.7%) or top-10 (1.17 [0.87–1.57]; I2 = 95.8%) diagnostic accuracy. Regarding triage (Fig. 3), LLMs performed com￾parably to HCPs, with a relative accuracy of 1.01 (0.94–1.09; I2 = 94.0%).

Accuracy by study design. No prospective studies assessed diagnostic accuracy (Table 2). For both LLMs and HCPs, diagnostic accuracy varied significantly across study designs. For top-1 and top-3 diagnoses, the highest accuracy was observed in synthetic vignettes, followed by retro￾spective clinical cases and published case reports. The top-5 and top-10 diagnostic accuracy showed a similar trend. However, study design did not significantly affect the relative diagnostic accuracy of LLMs versus HCPs (all p > 0.05). For triage accuracy, 3 studies employed a prospective design. No significant differences were found in triage accuracy across study designs for LLMs or HCPs, nor in the relative accuracy of LLMs compared with HCPs (all p > 0.05).

Accuracy by LLM type. Relative diagnostic and triage accuracy of LLMs compared to HCPs varied substantially across models (Fig. 4). For top-1 relative diagnostic accuracy, values ranged from 0.33 (0.17–0.66) for Gemini 1.5 Flash to 1.88 (1.13–3.10) for Claude 3 Opus. For top-3 accuracy, the range was 0.47 (0.23–0.96) for GPT-4V to 1.33 (1.01– 1.74) for AIME. Top-5 accuracy ranged from 0.72 (0.50–1.04) for Google Bard to 1.33 (1.03–1.72) for AIME, while top-10 accuracy ranged from 0.70 (0.45–1.09) for Google Bard to 1.69 (1.31–2.20)for PhenoBrain. Relative triage accuracy varied from 0.77 (0.63–0.94) for GPT-3 to 1.10 (1.09–1.11) for GPT-4o. Iterative improvement within model series was evident, with GPT-4 out￾performing its predecessors in all relative accuracy metrics.

Other subgroup analyses. As shown in Table S1, HCP experience significantly influenced top-1 relative diagnostic accuracy, with LLMs performing comparable to junior HCPs (0.95 [0.80–1.14]) but worse than senior HCPs (0.77 [0.63–0.94], p = 0.030). Relative diagnostic accuracy of LLMs versus HCPs was slightly higher in non-urgent cases than in urgent cases, whereas relative triage accuracy was higher in urgent cases than in non-urgent cases, although these differences were not statistically significant.

Only one study compared different case modalities for top-1 diagnosis51, and another for top-3 diagnosis30. LLMs performed sub￾stantially worse than HCPs when only images were available, but showed greater improvement than HCPs when text was added. In one study comparing top-3 diagnostic accuracy across modality, LLMs outperformed HCPs when only text was provided, but underperformed when images were included30. Another study examined the effect of including examination results for top-3 diagnosis, finding that both LLMs and HCPs performed better with examination results, while the relative diagnostic accuracy remained similar47.

### Diagnostic accuracy of HCPs with versus without LLM assistance

Overall accuracy comparison. Nine studies evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of HCPs with and without LLM assistance24,28,30,31,34,35,48,49,56. As shown in Fig. 5, HCPs supported by LLMs outperformed those working alone. The relative diagnostic accuracy was 1.13 (1.00–1.27; I2 = 60.7%) for top-1 diagnoses, 1.11 (1.01–1.23; I2 = 61.5%) for top-3, 1.42 (1.16–1.73) for top-5, and 1.33 (0.94–1.87; I2 = 71.0%) for top-10 diagnoses.

Accuracy by study design. After stratifying by study design (Table S2), HCPs performed better on retrospective clinical cases than on published case reports, regardless of LLM assistance. Notably, LLM assistance provided greater improvement for top-1 diagnosis in published case reports compared with retrospective clinical cases (relative accuracy 1.49 [1.04–2.12] vs 1.03 [0.97–1.09]; p = 0.004).

Accuracy by LLM type. The effect of LLM assistance on HCP diagnostic accuracy varied across different models (Fig. S1). For top-1 diagnoses, relative accuracy ranged from 1.01 (0.93–1.09) for GPT-4o to 2.15 (1.28–3.63) for DeepSeek R1. For top-3 diagnoses, it ranged from 1.00 (0.95–1.07) for GPT-4o to 1.42 (1.13–1.79) for AIME. Top-5 accuracy was reported in a single study, showing a relative accuracy of 1.42 (1.16–1.73) for AIME. For top-10 diagnoses, relative accuracy was 1.08 (0.79–1.49) for PhenoBrain and 1.54 (1.27–1.87) for AIME.

## Quality assessment and sensitivity analysis

Quality assessment summary. The summary plot of methodological quality, risk of bias, and applicability concerns across studies is sum￾marized in Figs. S2 and S3. In the participant selection domain, 29 studies (58%) were rated as high or unclear risk of bias due to inappropriate sampling. In the index test domain, 28 studies (56%) clearly described LLM versions, prompts, and blinding procedures. Eighteen studies (36%) showed low risk of bias in the reference standard domain, with a clearly defined reference standard, evaluators, and blinded assessment pro￾cesses. For flow and timing, 29 studies (58%) were rated as low risk, with a well-documented timeframe for case acquisition and LLM access.

Regarding applicability concerns, 29 studies (58%) presented great concerns in participant selection due to non-consecutive or non￾random recruitment or inappropriate exclusion criteria. Forty studies (80%) raised great concerns in the index test domain, reflecting differ￾ences between LLM inputs and real-world clinical settings. Sixteen studies (32%) showed great or unclear concerns in the reference stan￾dard domain, mainly due to incomplete diagnostic criteria or insufficient reporting of evaluator qualifications.

Significant publication bias was detected in the top-1 relative diagnostic accuracy of HCPs with LLM assistance compared to HCPs alone (p = 0.018), while no publication bias was observed in other relative diag￾nostic or triage accuracy metrics (Fig. S4).

Sensitivity analysis by study risk of bias. For LLMs versus HCPs (Table S3), pooled estimates for top-1, top-3, top-5 diagnosis, and triage accu￾racy did not differ significantly between studies with low versus high/unclear risk of bias (all p > 0.05). However, for the top-10 diagnoses, statistically significant subgroup differences were observed when strati￾fying by participant selection (p = 0.019). For HCPs with versus without LLM assistance (Table S4), no significant subgroup differences were found for top-1 and top-3 diagnoses across any of the four risk of bias domains except for a borderline difference in the reference standard domain for top-1 diagnosis (p = 0.050). Sensitivity analyses for top-5 and top-10 diag￾noses were not performed due to insufficient numbers of studies (n = 1 and n = 2, respectively).

Sensitivity analysis for correlation assumptions. Sensitivity analyses comparing correlation coefficients (ρ = 0.8 vs. ρ = 0.5) for paired out￾comes produced consistent pooled estimates across all metrics. A lower correlation redistributed variance from the observation level to the study level, without changing the pooled estimates.

## Discussion

We found that LLMs currently lag behind HCPs in top-1 diagnostic accu￾racy, yet they show promise in generating differential diagnoses and assisting clinicians to improve their diagnostic performance. The triage accuracy of LLMs was comparable to that of HCPs. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously due to study heterogeneity, risk of bias, and gaps between controlled evaluations and real-world workflows. In light of persistent methodological deficiencies, this study also proposes aframework to guide future research.

The enduring challenge of achieving diagnostic excellence has dri￾ven decades of innovation, from clinician-facing Computerized Diag￾nostic Decision Support Systems (CDDS) to layperson-oriented Symptom Checkers (SC)61,62. These systems, typically relying on rule￾based logic or Bayesian inference63,64, have shown stagnant performance over time65,66, with SCs rarely exceeding 38% top-1 accuracy67,68. While direct comparisons are limited by differing datasets, the LLMs in our analysis achieved a pooled top-1 accuracy of 56.74%, suggesting a potential advantage over traditional algorithms by leveraging probabil￾istic patterns learned from vast, unstructured training data69,70. However, LLM performance generally falls short of HCPs (relative accuracy 0.89),

Notably, the relative diagnostic accuracy of LLMs compared to HCPs improved progressively as the diagnostic range widened, from 0.89 at top-1 to 1.17 at top-10. This pattern aligns with observations by Yang et al., who found that broader differential outputs can offer considerable guiding value even when the top diagnosis is incorrect57. Given that clinical reasoning requires iterative hypothesis refinement rather than static retrieval, LLMs may be more valuable as cognitive augmentation tools that expand diagnostic consideration, while leaving ultimate judg￾ment to clinicians. The observed improvement in HCP diagnostic per￾formance when assisted by LLMs, compared with unassisted conditions in our analysis, supports the promise of such collaborative approaches.

Beyond overall performance, the diagnostic accuracy of LLMs, HCPs, and their collaboration varied by study design. Across the three evaluation modes, accuracy generally ranked highest for synthetic vignettes, inter￾mediate for retrospective clinical cases, and lowest for published case reports, reflecting differences in information structure and diagnostic complexity. Synthetic vignettes are well-organized with complete infor￾mation and clear diagnostic clues, whereas retrospective clinical cases contain noise, missing data, and non-standardized documentation that better mirror clinical uncertainty5. Published case reports often feature rare or atypical presentations with educational value. Although absolute accu￾racy declined across these study designs, the relative difference between LLMs and HCPs remained stable, with both showing similar reductions from synthetic to clinical to published cases, suggesting shared limitations in handling diagnostic ambiguity. LLM assistance appeared more beneficial in published case reports, possibly due to its broader differential generation for rare presentations. However, existing studies rely heavily on artificial or highly selected scenarios, which may systematically over- or underestimate real-world performance. Retrospective clinical cases offer greater realism but remain limited by their historical nature, inability to capture dynamic, high-pressure decision-making, and susceptibility to documentation biases. The few randomized controlled trials conducted to date show mixed results71,72, further emphasizing the need for more prospective designs embedded in authentic clinical workflows to accurately assess diagnostic reliability and utility.

To integrate LLMs effectively within clinical workflows, several vari￾ables require systematic investigation. Most studies utilized text-based inputs, with insufficient head-to-head comparisons across modalities. Available evidence shows that adding text to image-based cases dis￾proportionately benefited LLMs, whereas adding images to text-based cases favored HCP performance30,51. This pattern suggests LLMs, primarily driven by natural language processing, excel at synthesizing large-scale textual information but perform less effectively in visual pattern recognition73. The modest performance of multimodal models like GPT-4V and Gemini Pro Vision further confirms current limitations in visual perception compared to experienced clinicians. Conclusions derived from text-based evaluations should therefore be cautiously extrapolated to visually-intensive specialties such as dermatology or radiology. The timing of LLM integration within diagnostic processes also warrants attention. One study found that incor￾porating examination results increased accuracyfor both LLMs and HCPs47, consistent with previous research74, but the relative performance of LLMs versus HCPs remained comparable. However, clinical diagnosis unfolds as a dynamic process rather than a single moment of complete information delivery. The sequential introduction of clinical data, from initial symptoms through various diagnostic tests, may differentially impact LLM and human clinician performance, an area requiring further investigation. Moreover, cognitive psychology research shows that clinicians develop initial diag￾nostic hypotheses early in patient encounters, subsequently shaping data collection strategies and diagnostic conclusions75,76. Understanding how and when to introduce LLM assistance becomes critical to enhancing clinical reasoning while avoiding the pitfalls of anchoring or confirmation bias.

Determining which clinicians benefit most from LLM assistance involves a complex interplay rather than simple performance comparisons. Our findings showed that LLMs performed comparably to junior clinicians but fell short of senior expertise. However, this experimental observation may not translate directly to clinical practice. Real-world diagnostic encounters require clinicians to elicit and synthesize information from patients who present with vague complaints, non-specific symptoms, and complex comorbidities. Junior clinicians, despite potentially gaining more from algorithmic assistance in controlled settings, may lack the sophisti￾cated history-taking and examination skills needed to optimally frame clinical problems for LLM consultation. This creates a paradox where those most likely to benefit from assistance may be least equipped to leverage it effectively. Human-AI collaboration is further complicated by clinician attitudes and self-perception. Clinicians often express concerns about professional autonomy erosion, liability risks, and the reliability of AI￾generated recommendations77–79. More experienced clinicians with greater confidence in their own judgment tend to be less receptive to AI recom￾mendations, potentially negating benefits from even highly accurate systems80. One study found that despite LLM alone outperforming HCPs, clinician performance did not improve with LLM assistance71. These dynamics underscore that successful clinical integration depends not only on algorithm performance but on careful alignment with clinician expertise levels, workflow design, and organizational readiness.

LLMs demonstrated triage capabilities comparable to HCPs (relative accuracy 1.01), indicating their greater proficiency in probabilistic classifi￾cation than complex causal reasoning68,81. LLMs performed slightly better in urgent cases, consistent with prior research on both LLMs and symptom checkers65,67,82. Such capability is crucial for acute or life-threatening con￾ditions, where distinguishing urgentfrom non-urgent care needs supersedes definitive diagnosis. We found no studies that examined LLM-assisted triage by HCPs, contrasting with diagnostic evaluations where collaboration improved accuracy. This gap limits understanding of whether LLMs might enhance triage decisions when integrated as cognitive aids rather than standalone tools, particularly in resource-constrained or high-volume set￾tings where decision support could be valuable. Future research should investigate strategies for combining LLMs and clinicians to fully leverage their joint potential in triage.

Substantial performance variability has been observed across LLMs in both standalone and assistance settings. Some models, such as Claude 3 Opus, AIME, and PhenoBrain, have shown strong performance, but they were evaluated in only one or a few studies and should be interpreted with caution. The GPT series is the most extensively studied, showing consistent improvement across model iterations and enhanced performance within the same model as the diagnostic scope expands. Performance variability across LLMs arises from differences in training data, architectures, and parameter scales, yet also indicates ongoing refinement of these components over time. Establishing transparent reporting standards is essential to enable standardized evaluation and comparison across studies and models. Con￾tinuous evaluation is also needed to monitor performance, identify emer￾ging capabilities and biases to inform both regulatory oversight and future model development. For institutions considering LLM adoption, this variability highlights the importance of rigorous local validation to align with specific contexts rather than relying solely on external evidence. Recent studies found that LLMs are susceptible to data-poisoning attacks, as even 0.001% of training tokens containing medical misinformation can sig￾nificantly increase the risk of hallucination83,84. Moreover, poor explain￾ability of LLM decision-making undermines clinician and patient trust85. In the long term, enhancing model reliability and safety through knowledge graph integration83, architectural innovation86,87, and explainability techni￾ques that make model reasoning trasparent88,89will be critical to realizing the full clinical value of LLMs.

Sensitivity analysis by risk of bias indicates that our primary findings remain generally robust, though fluctuations in top-10 diagnostic accuracy may reflect either genuine differences or the limited number of studies for analysis. However, pervasive methodological deficiencies across participant selection, index test, reference standard, and study flow reveal the nascent state of the field. Coupled with potential publication bias favoring positive results, these quality gaps highlight an urgent need for standardized design and reporting. To this end, we propose a framework structured around four components: study design and settings, participants and data collection, index test and reference standard, and comparators and outcomes to inform further studies (Table 3).

For study design and settings, methodological rigor and transparent reporting are essential for ensuring validity and generalizability, even within the practical and resource constraints of prospective randomized trials. A recurrent concern is the inconsistent use of basic design ter￾minology. Several studies inaccurately labeled retrospective analyses as prospective, even when data collection occurred after reference standards were established25. In LLM-based diagnostic research, truly prospective design requires data collection initiated before both index test and

reference standard90, as this distinction directly influences spectrum bias, data leakage, and real-world applicability. Target clinical settings also require a precise definition. Real-world practice differs from standardized examinations with predefined answers, motivating our restriction to studies using open-ended questions that better reflect actual diagnostic reasoning. However, most studies evaluated LLMs using summarized case presentations, whereas authentic clinical interactions typically involve multi-turn dialogs. Evidence suggests LLM performance drops significantly when information is fragmented across exchanges rather than integrated summaries91. This gap between current research meth￾odologies and clinical reality warrants systematic investigation.

Current studies provide insufficient documentation of participant recruitment and data collection procedures. Future studies should specify data origins in detail, including geographic location and clinical settings for newly acquired cases, or identifying specific electronic health records, disease registries, or public repositories for extracted data. The temporal relationship between data collection and original generation must be clarified, as prior inclusion in LLM training corpora could compromise model independence. Personnel responsible for data extraction and quality control should be identified to ensure accountability and potential handling bias. Methodologically, inclusion and exclusion cri￾teria require explicit documentation with clear descriptions of sampling approaches, whether consecutive, random, or convenience-based enrollment. Dataset composition must delineate included clinical infor￾mation (chief complaints, medical histories, laboratory results, imaging reports) and input modality (text-only versus multimodal). The language used and patient demographics should be reported. Data acquisition instruments, preprocessing procedures, quality control measures, and missing data management strategies are necessary for study replication and result interpretation.

For the index test, the generative nature of LLMs and their rapid iteration necessitate comprehensive technical documentation. Studies must report the base model’s name and version, accessibility method (public API or local deployment), knowledge cutoff date, and timing of test adminis￾tration. Text generation processes should detail any fine-tuning or align￾ment with prompt engineering (e.g., zero-shot, few-shot, chain-of-thought). Inference parameters, including temperature, maximum token length, repetition penalties, and random seed settings, require specification. Reference standard selection demands clear justification with granularity matching the clinical task under evaluation. To avoid incorporation bias, the reference standard must be established independently of LLM outputs. Given that LLM-generated content inevitably requires subjective human assessment, studies should report assessor qualifications, demographic composition, and inter-rater reliability measures.

In terms of comparators and outcomes, studies comparing or colla￾borating with humans offer greater value than isolated LLM testing. For human-LLM comparison studies, descriptions should include participant numbers, specialties, experience levels, and consensus-reaching processes. Information provided to human participants and LLMs must be equivalent to ensure a valid comparison, with clear documentation of whether clin￾icians accessed external references. In human-LLM collaboration studies, authors should describe interaction workflows, including the sequence of interaction, user interface design, LLM suggestion presentation methods, and final clinical decision recording procedures. Appropriate metrics for evaluating generative outputs remain an open question. We selected accu￾racy as the primary outcome because it reflects core clinical competence while remaining relatively objective and quantifiable. For tasks producing differential diagnosis lists, reporting multiple top-k accuracy levels provides a more informative assessment than a single-point metric. Beyond accuracy, multiple additional dimensions merit evaluation91,92, including explanation quality (e.g., factual consistency, hallucination rates, output stability), error pattern analysis to identify disease-specific or subgroup-related issues, and fairness assessment addressing bias amplification and the generalizability of English-centric evaluations to other languages and low-resource settings.

The integration of LLMs into healthcare raises ethical con￾siderations demanding rigorous governance. Data privacy concerns arise as LLMs involve sensitive medical information with re￾identification risks93. Prevention requires tiered, risk-based regula￾tion, including cross-examination for data leakage, adversarial pene￾tration testing, and privacy-utility trade-off benchmarking94. Accountability frameworks must establish that final decision-making authority resides with qualified healthcare professionals, with audi￾table records enabling retrospective review of LLM recommendations and well-defined liability boundaries in human-AI collaboration95. Bias in training data related to gender, race, and geography may yield inequitable diagnostic or triage recommendations96–98. Monitoring performance disparities across demographic subgroups, incorporating diverse multi-population datasets, and conducting regular bias audits can help mitigate these concerns. Furthermore, automation bias poses risks as physicians may uncritically accept LLM outputs, potentially leading to skill degradation99,100. Medical education must evolve to balance technical literacy in understanding LLM capabilities and limitations with humanistic competencies, including empathy and contextual communication, ensuring technology augments rather than replaces the patient-physician relationship. Regulatory oversight of LLMs is more complex than for traditional AI medical devices due to their generative and versatile nature. Public-private partnerships and international cooperation should offer pathways to bridge regional gaps in accessibility and affordability, ensuring these technologies advance global health equity rather than exacerbating inequalities across diverse healthcare systems.

This study has several limitations. First, we excluded preprint articles to ensure peer-reviewed quality, potentially missing recent publications, though our search covered seven databases through September 2025 to maximize literature coverage. Second, from a methodological perspective, the unavailability of individual participant data precluded the calculation of exact within-study correlations for paired outcomes. We addressed this by constructing a variance–covariance matrix with assumed correlations and confirmed robustness through sensitivity analyses, yet this approach remains an approximation of the true dependency structure. Furthermore, although we modeled dataset-level clustering to account for shared benchmarks, our ability to identify all data overlaps was constrained by the reporting quality of primary studies, potentially leading to residual non￾independence from unidentified shared cases. Third, the limited number of comparative studies examining top-5 and top-10 diagnostic accuracy for HCPs with or without LLM assistance may yield unstable pooled estimates. Insufficient primary studies and inadequate reporting of key variables such as case modality and examination results constrained our subgroup analyses and ability to identify performance-influencing factors. Fourth, substantial heterogeneity persisted despite subgroup analyses, likely reflecting varia￾tions in study design, patient populations, and LLM implementations. Fifth, some included studies exhibited a high risk of bias, and while sensitivity analyses demonstrated robustness of main findings, caution is needed when interpreting results. Finally, publication bias and potential conflicts of interest should also be considered, as financial incentives tied to LLM development may influence study outcomes. Future research should prioritize transparent funding disclosure and standardized methodologies to enable robust comparisons across studies and healthcare settings.

This systematic review provides a comprehensive yet cautious assess￾ment of LLM capabilities in clinical diagnosis and triage. Current evidence suggests potential value as assistive tools, yet the predominance of retro￾spective designs and curated cases creates substantial uncertainty about real￾world performance. Considerable heterogeneity across models and tasks indicates these systems require individualized evaluation rather than broad performance generalization. Moving forward, the field requires prospective evaluations embedded within actual clinical workflows, rigorous metho￾dological standards, and transparent reporting of both capabilities and limitations. Equally important are unresolved challenges surrounding data privacy, algorithmic bias, accountability frameworks, and preservation of clinical judgment. Responsible integration depends not solely on techno￾logical capability, but on sustained multidisciplinary collaboration to establish evidence-based standards that genuinely enhance care while safeguarding patient safety.

## Methods

The study protocol was registered with PROSPERO, the international prospective register of systematic reviews (CRD42024621023), and con￾ducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines (Supplementary Note 1).

### Search strategy and eligibility criteria

A systematic search was performed across seven databases, including PubMed, Embase, IEEE Xplore, Cochrane Library, Epistemonikos, CINAHL, and PsycINFO for studies published between 1st January 2020 and 10th September 2025, without language restrictions. Medical Subject Headings terms and keywords related to LLM, diagnosis, triage, and accuracy were used to identify studies comparing the diagnostic or triage accuracy of LLMs, HCPs, or their combined performance. The full search strategy is provided in Supplementary Note 2. Manual searches of biblio￾graphies, citations, and related articles of the included studies were also conducted to capture any relevant studies missed during the initial search.

Eligibility was independently assessed by two reviewers, who screened titles and abstracts and resolved discrepancies through discussion and consensus. Studies were included if they involved inputting symptoms and biodata (i.e., age and gender) into LLMs to generate diagnostic or triage recommendations, which were then compared with those provided by HCPs, or if they compared HCP performance with and without LLM assistance. Inclusion criteria were independent of the target population, disease of interest, or LLMs used. Studies were excluded if they: (1) required LLMs or HCPs to select correct diagnoses from multiple-choice questions rather than generating possible diagnoses; (2) did not directly present or were unable to calculate the number of correctly diagnosed or triaged cases; or (3) involved non-human or animal subjects. Review articles, editorials, comments, conference abstracts, and preprints were also excluded.

### Data extraction

To ensure consistency and comprehensiveness in our overview of the cur￾rent LLM evaluation landscape, two reviewers independently extracted data from all included studies using a structured, pre-piloted data extraction form. This form captured key information across five domains: study characteristics (first author, year, country, scenario, specialty, and study design); case characteristics (number, source, eligibility criteria, patient demographics, case modality, examination result availability, text language, and reference standard); LLM implementation details (model(s) used and test period); comparators; and primary outcome data on diagnostic or triage accuracy. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion to reach a consensus.

Study designs were categorized into four types according to case source and data collection methods, mapping a progression from simulated to real￾world evidence: (1) Synthetic vignettes, which used artificially generated patient cases; (2) Published case reports, which sourced cases from publicly available educational materials such as journal case challenges; (3) Retro￾spective clinical cases, which used real-world patient data collected from electronic health records or other databases; and (4) Prospective clinical cases, which involved real-time data collection and consecutive case enrollment in clinical settings.

The primary outcome was diagnostic or triage accuracy of LLMs compared to HCPs. The secondary outcome was HCP performance with versus without LLM assistance. Diagnostic accuracy was assessed using top￾k metrics (k = 1, 3, 5, 10), as these measures are commonly adopted in the literature69, assess both direct and differential diagnosis performance, and were most consistently reported across included studies for pooled analysis. Triage accuracy was based on whether the acuity of the medical issue was correctly identified, leading to appropriate patient referrals.

### Quality assessment

We developed a domain-specific risk assessment framework by adapting the Quality Assessment of Studies of Diagnostic Accuracy-Revised (QUADAS-2) to align with our research objectives107,108. The modified tool retained QUADAS-2’s core structure, comprising two parts: risk of bias assessment and applicability evaluation. The risk of bias assessment evaluated four domains (participant selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing) using several signaling questions per domain, with responses aggregated to determine categorical risk classi￾fications (high, unclear, or low). The applicability assessment employed one signaling question for each of the first three domains from the risk of bias part. Two independent reviewers conducted the assessments, resolving disagreements through discussion. The detailed framework, including signaling questions and corresponding explanations, is pro￾vided in Supplementary Note 3.

### Data analysis

We used multilevel random-effects models to pool the relative diagnostic or triage accuracies of LLMs compared to HCPs, as well as the relative accuracies of HCPs with and without LLM assistance109. This modeling strategy was selected to account for the hierarchical and correlated structure of the data, including overlapping case sources, shared control groups, and multiple LLMs evaluated within the same study. Rather than averaging results across multiple LLMs within a study, each individual LLM–HCP comparison was treated as a separate observation, thereby preserving thefull variability in model performance and avoiding bias introduced by within￾study aggregation.

For comparisons between LLMs and HCPs, we fitted three-level hierarchical models. Random effects were specified at the dataset level to account for shared case sources across studies, at the study level to capture between-study heterogeneity, and at the observation level to model the evaluation of multiple LLMs within the same study. For comparisons between HCPs with and without LLM assistance, model complexity was adapted to the structure of the available data. Random effects were specified at the dataset level when overlapping case sources were present and at the study level to account for between-study variability.

To address the paired and correlated outcomes arising from evalua￾tions on the same clinical cases and from shared HCP control groups, we incorporated a variance–covariance matrix adjustment into all relevant models. Sampling error correlations were specified using an assumed within-study correlation coefficient (ρ = 0.8), reflecting the strong depen￾dency expected when different models are evaluated on identical case sets. Sensitivity analyses were performed using a more conservative correlation (ρ = 0.5), and results were compared to assess the robustness of pooled estimates.

Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test, with variance components decomposed across hier￾archical levels where applicable110. To explore potential sources of hetero￾geneity, across-study subgroup analyses were conducted according to study design and LLM type by specifying these variables as moderators within the multilevel meta-analysis. For the LLM type, effect estimates from evalua￾tions of the same model across different studies were pooled. In addition, within-study subgroup analyses were performed for HCP experience, case urgency, case modality, and inclusion of examination results. These analyses were restricted to studies reporting internal stratification for the corre￾sponding variables and were considered exploratory and hypothesis￾generating because of the limited number of contributing studies111.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted based on study risk of bias across four domains (participant selection, index tests, reference standard, flow, and timing) to assess the robustness of the pooled results. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots, which provide a visual assessment of asymmetry in effect size distribution that may indicate bias, and Egger’s regression test, which statistically detects funnel plot asymmetry by testing whether smaller studies show systematically different effects than larger ones112. All analyses were conducted using the metafor package (rma.mv function) in R (version 4.5.1), with a two-sided significance threshold of p < 0.05. Forest plots andfunnel plots were generated using R (version 4.5.1), and risk of bias plots were generated using RevMan (version 5.3). All figures were then assembled and annotated in Adobe Illustrator.
