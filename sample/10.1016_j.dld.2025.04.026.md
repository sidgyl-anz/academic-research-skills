Background and Aims: Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD) are chronic conditions that can lead to a phys￾ical, social, and economic burden. Generative artificial intelligence (AI), particularly ChatGPT, gained at￾tention for its potential to support medical practice. However, concerns remain about the reliability and consistency of its responses. This study systematically reviews the existing evidence on the role of gen￾erative AI in IBD.

Materials and methods: We conducted a systematic literature review following PRISMA guidelines. Studies investigating generative AI in IBD care were identified through PubMed and Embase (Jan 2020–Sep 2024).

Results: From 2875 records, 8 studies (2023–2024) met inclusion criteria: 5 on patient education, 2 on decision support, and 1 on research ideation. For patient education, ChatGPT provided clear and accurate responses, with accuracy reaching 84.2 % in a study, though sometimes lacked consistency. In decision support, ChatGPT’s classifications of ulcerative colitis severity aligned with clinician assessments in 80 % of cases and in 87.8 % of cases for guideline-based dysplasia management. For research ideation, ChatGPT generated highly relevant (mean score: 4.9 ± 0.26) and clear (4.8 ± 0.41) questions, but lacked specificity (2.86/5) and originality (1.07/5).

Conclusions: Generative AI shows promise in IBD care, but concerns about accuracy, consistency, and outdated information highlight the need for expert oversight before clinical integration.

## 1. Introduction

Inflammatory Bowel Diseases (IBD), comprising Crohn’s dis￾ease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), are chronic gastrointesti￾nal disorders affecting millions of people worldwide [1,2], with a prevalence that is steadily increasing [3,4]. These conditions are characterized by an immune-mediated inflammation of the diges￾tive tract, leading to typical intestinal symptoms (abdominal pain, diarrhoea, haematochezia), with potential systemic involvement (anaemia, fever, fatigue, weight loss) and sometimes to severe complications (colorectal cancer, hospitalization, surgery) [1,2]. Moreover, IBD often result in a substantial long-term disability, encompassing not only physical impairments but also limitations in social and economic participation, affecting education, employ￾ment, and overall psychosocial functioning [5]. The complex na￾ture of IBD, with their varied presentations and unpredictable dis￾ease course, poses significant challenges in diagnosis, treatment, and long-term management. The literature is often not able to pro￾vide clear-cut evidence in crucial situations such as drug position￾ing, sequencing or optimal strategies for managing specific patient populations, including elderly patients, those with comorbidities, or pregnant women [6–10]. As a consequences international guide￾lines are not conclusive if not frankly contradictory [11,12].

In recent years, the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) technology has opened new avenues for innovation in health￾care and to improve the quality of care. Particularly, generative AI, including large language models (LLMs) and chatbots (e.g. Chat￾GPT), has shown promising applications across various medical fields [13]. These AI systems can generate human-like text, analyse vast amounts of data, and provide insights that could potentially revolutionize the physician-patient relationship [14].

The intersection of generative AI and IBD management repre￾sents a promising frontier in gastroenterology. Potential applica￾tions range from analysing large volumes of clinical data to en￾hancing diagnostic accuracy, predicting disease flares, and person￾alizing communication between healthcare providers and patients, as well as tailoring treatment plans and improving patient educa￾tion.

For instance, in the future, AI-powered chatbots could offer con￾tinuous, round-the-clock support to patients, offering guidance on symptom management and medication adherence. LLMs could also assist clinicians in interpreting the ever-expanding medical litera￾ture and knowledge, facilitating evidence-based decision-making.

Additionally, clinicians are faced with vast amounts of unstructured written data—such as emails, visit notes, and patient records— that AI could efficiently synthesize, categorize, and summarize, helping streamline workflows and improve clinical efficiency.

Moreover, generative AI could accelerate research by analysing large datasets and generating hypotheses to investigate in clinical studies [15].

However, the implementation of AI in healthcare comes with its own set of challenges. These include ensuring the accuracy and re￾liability of AI-generated information, addressing privacy and ethical concerns, and integrating these technologies into existing health￾care systems [16].

This systematic review aims to synthesize and critically evalu￾ate the current body of literature investigating the applications of generative AI in IBD. By doing so, we seek to identify promising areas of implementation, potential challenges, and gaps in current research. By critically examining both the opportunities and limi￾tations of generative AI in IBD, we aim to provide a balanced per￾spective that can guide future research and clinical applications in this rapidly evolving field.

## 2. Background on artificial intelligence and generative AI

AI is commonly described as the ability of machines to mimic human cognitive functions such as learning, reasoning, decision￾making and language understanding. Over time, AI evolved into various subfields, including machine learning (ML) and deep learn￾ing (DL). ML models, for instance, learn patterns from data to make predictions or classifications, often excelling in specific tasks but requiring extensive retraining when applied to new problems [17].

Generative AI, a more recent and revolutionary branch of AI, goes beyond analysing data or making simple predictions. It gen￾erates original content—such as text, images, or even music— by analysing and emulating the patterns and structures found in its training data. While older ML or DL predictive models mostly relied on supervised learning methods based on human￾labeled data and were limited to predicting outcomes based on past examples, generative AI models are trained on vast, diverse datasets encompassing enormous amounts of internet-scale data, enabling them to create internal representations of the underlying data and therefore generalize across tasks without domain-specific training [16].

This capability is driven by transformer-based architectures, which were discovered in 2017 [18], one of their key features be￾ing the ability to improve in performance by increasing the size of the model and size of the training dataset. This was exemplified recently by GPT-4, developed by OpenAI, which showed ground￾breaking performance across many domains [19]

Transformers allow models to capture relationships between data points, such as the context of words in a sentence, enabling them to generate coherent and rich outputs. These architectures, combined with massive datasets, empower generative AI to pro￾duce results that are both flexible and creative, marking a signifi￾cant departure from earlier, task-specific AI systems [16,20,21].

## 3. Methods

### 3.1. Literature search details

A systematic review of the literature was conducted in ac￾cordance with the PRISMA guidelines [22]. The review protocol was prepared in accordance with the PRISMA-P guidelines [23] and registered on Open Science Framework (doi:10.17605/OSF.IO/ 74RVN).

A comprehensive search of PubMed and Embase was conducted to identify studies published between 2020 and 2024 reporting on the use of generative AI in IBD field.

Search terms for inflammatory bowel disease and artificial in￾telligence were used. The bibliographies of included studies were also hand-searched to identify other relevant studies not identi￾fied by our search. No language restrictions were used during the search. The precise search strings used for PubMed and Embase are available in the supplementary materials.

### 3.2. Eligibility criteria

Studies meeting all the following criteria were considered for inclusion: 1) full-text papers focusing on applications of genera￾tive AI (including LLMs, chatbots, ChatGPT) in IBD; 2) peer-reviewd original articles published in English from 2020 onwards 3)stud￾ies involving human subjects or human-derived data. Studies not specifically focused on IBD (e.g., general gastrointestinal disorders without separate IBD analysis) or involving AI techniques that are not considered generative (e.g., traditional ML or DL models) were excluded.

### 3.3. Data extraction

Two reviewers (FL, GPLR), after elimination of duplicate records, independently screened titles and abstracts of records identified by the literature search to identify potentially relevant studies. Dis￾agreements were resolved by discussion with other reviewers (SM, DS). Records identified by title and abstract screening then under￾went full-text review to evaluate eligibility for final inclusion by at least two reviewers.

For each included study data were extracted by at least two re￾viewers on study characteristics, patient characteristics, generative AI characteristics, intervention details, outcomes, limitations and future directions.

### 3.4. Outcomes

We aimed to evaluate the following outcomes: 1) the current and potential applications of generative AI technologies in the management of IBD, including but not limited to diagnosis, treat￾ment planning, patient education, and research; 2) the quality and strength of evidence supporting the use of generative AI in IBD management. 3) Additionally, we provided a conceptual evaluation of the potential benefits and risks associated with implementing generative AI technologies in IBD care, based on the existing liter￾ature and contextual insights.

### 3.5. Study quality and risk of bias

The quality of included studies and the risk of bias were as￾sessed independently for each study by two reviewers (FL and SM). Disagreements were resolved through discussion or, if necessary, consultation with a third reviewer.

The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used for observational studies. This tool evaluates studies on three main domains (selec￾tion, comparability, outcome). Each domain was rated using a star system, with a maximum of nine stars possible, indicating higher methodological quality.

## 4. Results

Given the limited number of studies (n = 8) and the variabil￾ity in methodologies, outcome measures, and AI tools used across studies, a meta-analysis was not feasible. Instead, a systematic re￾view was conducted to summarize and synthesize the available ev￾idence.

### 4.1. Characteristics of included studies

Literature search identified 2875 records. Among these, after screening we identified 8 studies examining the application of gen￾erative AI in IBD that met the eligibility criteria (Fig. 1) [24–31]. Table 1 shows the characteristics of the 8 included studies. All in￾cluded papers were observational studies, published between 2023 and 2024. In terms of geographical distribution, three studies were conducted in Israel, two in the USA, and one each in Turkey, Italy, and Malta.

### 4.2. Applications of generative AI in IBD

Through the analysis of the studies, three primary areas of AI application in IBD care were identified: patient education and communication, clinical decision support, and research ideation and support. Based on their objectives and findings, the studies were categorized as follows: five studies (62.5 %) focused on pa￾tient education and communication, two studies (25 %) on clinical decision support, and one study (12.5 %) on research ideation and support.

• Patient Education and Communication

The largest group of studies (five studies) [26–29,31] focused on evaluating ChatGPT’s effectiveness in providing accurate and com￾prehensible responses to patient inquiries.

• Sciberras et al., [31] analyzed responses from ChatGPT 3.5 to 38 patient questions related to IBD and assessed accuracy and completeness based on ECCO guidelines. Fourteen expert gas￾troenterologists in IBD evaluated the responses, with 84.2 % scoring 4 or higher on a 5-point Likert scale for accuracy, yield￾ing a mean score of 3.87 ± 0.6. Completeness scores indicated that 34.2 % of responses were rated fully complete (3 on a 3-point scale), while 55.3 % were rated as partially complete (scores between 2 and <3).

• Kerbage et al., [27] focused on a broader set of 20 IBD-related questions using ChatGPT-4, evaluated by three gastroenterolo￾gists using a granular accuracy scale. Results showed that Chat￾GPT was 100 % accurate for 55 % of questions, 40 % were par￾tially accurate, and 5 % accurate but with missing details. When viewed from a patient perspective, responses were deemed suitable for patient use in 75 % of cases. However, 50 % of re￾sponses lacked suitable references, and another 15 % had un￾suitable references, highlighting challenges in verifying sources.

• Naqvi et al., [29] investigated responses from three AI chatbot models—ChatGPT, BingChat, and YouChat—on six dietary man￾agement questions for IBD. Each question was posed to the models three times, resulting in 18 responses per model. Two gastroenterologists then evaluated the responses for accuracy and consistency. ChatGPT provided reliably appropriate answers in 83.3 % of cases, while BingChat and YouChat scored 66.7 %. Despite ChatGPT’s favorable performance in accuracy, inconsis￾tencies were noted in 3 of the 6 questions across iterations, suggesting limitations in replicable responses.

• Cankurtaran et al., [28] assessed ChatGPT-4’s responses to 20 IBD-related questions (10 on CD and 10 on UC, half of each set tailored to patients and the other half tailored to healthcare providers). Using a 1–7 Likert scale, the study evaluated reli￾ability and usefulness, finding average scores of 4.7 ± 1.26 for CD and 4.75 ± 1.06 for UC, according to the opinion of two gas￾troenterologists. Higher reliability and usefulness were reported for provider-directed questions (5.0 ± 1.21 and 5.15 ± 1.08, respectively), indicating ChatGPT’s enhanced performance on technical topics. The study highlighted strong inter-rater reli￾ability (Cronbach’s α > 0.9) across assessments, underscoring consistency in evaluators’ ratings.

• Gravina et al., [26] focused on ChatGPT 3.5’s ability to re￾spond accurately to 10 common patient questions about IBD, selected by a team of gastroenterologists. The study aimed to evaluate the responses’ accuracy, completeness, and rele￾vance based on current IBD guidelines. While ChatGPT gener￾ally provided accurate information, responses often lacked suffi￾cient detail—particularly in differentiating between CD and UC. For instance, dietary and therapeutic advice lacked specific nu￾ance, with limitations in evidence-based details regarding nu￾trition’s role and the use of topical therapy in UC. Moreover, ChatGPT’s responses missed updates on new treatment options like oral small molecules, and some responses were ambiguous, highlighting the need for professional oversight and up-to-date sources.

Overall, in this group of studies, researchers agree that gen￾erative AI, particularly ChatGPT, holds promise for patient educa￾tion and communication in the IBD field. Several studies found that ChatGPT could provide clear and accurate responses to pa￾tient inquiries, making it a valuable tool for enhancing patient un￾derstanding of their condition and treatment options. For instance, Sciberras et al. and Kerbage et al., [27] reported high accuracy rates (84.2 % and 55 %, respectively) in ChatGPT’s responses to IBD￾related questions, suggesting it could be an effective tool for deliv￾ering evidence-based information to patients.

However, despite these positive findings, there are notable lim￾itations. ChatGPT’s responses were often incomplete, with Sciber￾ras et al., [31] highlighting that only 34.2 % of responses were fully complete, and Kerbage et al., [27] noting that some answers missed critical details. • Clinical Decision Support

Two studies, by Levartovsky et al. and Ghersin et al., assessed the role of ChatGPT in clinical decision-making within IBD care [25,30].

The study by Levartovsky explored ChatGPT’s ability to classify UC severity and provide hospitalization recommendations based on Truelove and Witts criteria of 20 real cases. The study showed an 80 % concordance rate with gastroenterologist evaluation on severity, with an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.839. ChatGPT recommended hospitalization in more cases (16/18: 88.9 %), than what was observed in real-world clinical decisions (12/20: 60 %). For 2 cases with moderate UC, ChatGPT could not provide a deci￾sive recommendation.

Ghersin et al. conducted a comparative study evaluating Chat￾GPT’s performance in providing guideline-based recommendations for colorectal dysplasia screening, surveillance, and endoscopic management, compared to that of gastroenterologists (4 IBD spe￾cialists and 4 non-IBD specialists) across 30 clinical scenarios involving IBD patients. With an average correct response rate of 87.8 %, ChatGPT’s performance was not significantly different (p = 0.95) from that of human specialists (IBD specialist: 85.8 %, non-IBD specialist: 89.2 %).

Both studies agree that ChatGPT can be a useful tool in en￾hancing clinical decision-making, especially for increasing adher￾ence to established guidelines. It could also serve as a valuable re￾source for non-specialists, improving decision support in settings where gastroenterological expertise may be limited. However, a key limitation is that ChatGPT’s recommendations are based on pre-existing, filtered data from clinical scenarios and do not ac￾count for real-time patient interactions, such as physical exam￾inations, which are crucial for accurate clinical assessment and decision-making.

• Use of Generative AI in Research Ideation and Support

One study by Lahat et al. investigated the potential of Chat￾GPT to assist researchers in generating new research ideas in gas￾troenterology, including in the IBD field [24]. ChatGPT was asked to identify 5 relevant research questions in the IBD field. 3 gas￾troenterologists rated the questions (1–5 scale) based on rele￾vance, originality, clarity, and specificity. The study found that ChatGPT provided a variety of relevant (mean and standard de￾viation: 4.9 ± 0.26;) and clear (4.8 ± 0.41) research suggestions, though the specificity and originality (respectively 2.86 ± 0.64 and 1.07 ± 0.26) were sometimes suboptimal according to the expert’s opinion. On the other hand, the evaluation of ChatGPT’s perfor￾mance was based on subjective ratings by only 3 gastroenterolo￾gists. However, the high inter-rater reliability (ICC = 0.979) con￾firmed consistency across reviewers.

Therefore, the study suggests that ChatGPT can be a useful tool for generating research ideas in the IBD field. While the AI was able to produce relevant and clear research questions, its suggestions were sometimes lacking in originality and specificity. This highlights ChatGPT’s potential as a starting point for research ideation, but also underscores the need for expert refinement to ensure the highest quality of research questions.

## 5. Conclusion

This systematic review represents, to our knowledge, the first comprehensive evaluation of generative AI applications in the man￾agement of IBD, providing a critical assessment of its potential ad￾vantages and inherent limitations in clinical practice. Our findings highlight the diverse utility of generative AI across different do￾mains such as patient education, clinical decision support and re￾search ideation.

A well-known strength of generative AI lies in its ability to en￾hance patient education through the provision of tailored, accessi￾ble information [32,33]. This capability fosters greater patient en￾gagement and comprehension of disease management strategies.

However, variability in the accuracy, clarity, and completeness of AI-generated content, as noted in some studies [28,29,31], rein￾forces the necessity of positioning these technologies as comple￾mentary to, rather than substitutive of, clinician-led care to main￾tain clinical reliability and safeguard patient outcomes.

Similarly, in the realm of clinical decision support, genera￾tive AI demonstrated potential to assist in guideline-driven clin￾ical decision-making and in prognostic evaluation [25,30]. Nev￾ertheless, its application remains constrained by the inability to replicate the holistic clinical judgment of healthcare providers, which encompasses the integration of physical examination find￾ings and nuanced patient context. While AI’s capacity to synthe￾size vast and complex datasets offers a pathway toward more per￾sonalized approaches to care, its current limitations must not be overlooked.

The studies included in this review also reveal critical chal￾lenges that limit broader implementation. Predominantly, these in￾clude limited sample sizes and single-center observational study designs, which limit the generalizability of findings. Furthermore, the risk of bias of the studies was judged to be intermediate or high, with the quality of the studies not being optimal.

Another significant limitation identified is the reliance on out￾dated information. This issue likely arises from the temporal constraints of the training data, as developing new generative AI models requires a long time, so the temporal cut-off of the training data is usually outdated by 6–12 months by the time the AI model is released. For instance the GPT-4o models currently deployed in ChatGPT were first released in May 2024 and have a knowledge cut-off of October 2023 [34]. Consequently, these systems are cur￾rently unable to incorporate the latest guidelines published there￾after. This temporal limitation presents a significant challenge in the dynamic field of IBD management, where integration of the most current evidence is essential for effective clinical decision￾making. However, incorporation of search and retrieval augmented generation to provide the AI with relevant up to date information may address these issues in the future [35].

## 5. Conclusion (continued)

Similarly, ethical considerations represent a critical concern. In contexts such as patient interactions and clinical case analyses, un￾resolved issues related to data privacy, informed consent, and the potential influence of AI on clinical decision-making warrant ur￾gent attention [32,36]. Additionally, within the domain of research ideation, although generative AI demonstrated utility in tasks such as brainstorming and summarizing existing literature, its outputs frequently lacked originality [24]. This raises important questions about its capacity for genuine innovation and its alignment with ethical standards, particularly in matters of authorship and intel￾lectual property.

In conclusion, our findings emphasize that generative AI, while valuable as an adjunctive tool, cannot substitute the comprehen￾sive clinical evaluation performed by healthcare professionals. Such evaluations rely on the integration of physical examinations, de￾tailed patient histories, and real-time critical thinking. Moving for￾ward, addressing these limitations will require the development of more comprehensive training datasets, the implementation of ro￾bust ethical frameworks, and the establishment of clearly defined roles for AI within clinical and research contexts [37].
