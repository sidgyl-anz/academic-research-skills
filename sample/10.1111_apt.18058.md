Summary  
Background: Interest in large language models (LLMs), such as OpenAI's ChatGPT, across multiple specialties has grown as a source of patient-facing medical advice and provider-facing clinical decision support. The accuracy of LLM responses for gastro-enterology and hepatology-related questions is unknown.  
Aims: To evaluate the accuracy and potential safety implications for LLMs for the diagnosis, management and treatment of questions related to gastroenterology and hepatology.  
Methods: We conducted a systematic literature search including Cochrane Library, Google Scholar, Ovid Embase, Ovid MEDLINE, PubMed, Scopus and the Web of Science Core Collection to identify relevant articles published from inception until January 28, 2024, using a combination of keywords and controlled vocabulary for LLMs and gastroenterology or hepatology. Accuracy was defined as the percentage of entirely correct answers.  
Results: Among the 1671 reports screened, we identified 33 full-text articles on using LLMs in gastroenterology and hepatology and included 18 in the final analy-sis. The accuracy of question-responding varied across different model versions. For example, accuracy ranged from 6.4% to 45.5% with ChatGPT-3.5 and was between 40% and 91.4% with ChatGPT-4. In addition, the absence of standardised methodol-ogy and reporting metrics for studies involving LLMs places all the studies at a high risk of bias and does not allow for the generalisation of single-study results.  
Conclusions: Current general-purpose LLMs have unacceptably low accuracy on clinical gastroenterology and hepatology tasks, which may lead to adverse patient safety events through incorrect information or triage recommendations, which might overburden healthcare systems or delay necessary care.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Large language models (LLMs) are transformer-based neural net-works with billions of parameters trained on a broad text corpus derived from diverse sources (including scientific articles, books, and Internet content).1,2 The typical transformer neural network architecture (see Box 1 for a glossary of key terminology) creates meaningful embeddings using the attention mechanism. This ar-chitecture consists of encoders, which process input data into a context vector, and decoders, which translate the context vector into the desired output.2–8 Decoder-only LLMs, such as OpenAI's ChatGPT, are autoregressive models, indicating that the text-generation process predicts the next word using all preceding words, and the final outcome of the model is in a form that humans can readily recognize.9,10 This architecture enables decoder-only LLMs to receive natural language prompts, generate human-like responses, and perform a wide range of previously intractable tasks.

Decoder-only LLMs have the potential to overcome the produc-tivity paradox in healthcare since they are easy to use and rapidly improve.11–13 However, these systems are poorly understood and unreliable, with plausible-sounding outputs (termed hallucinations) that contain inaccuracies.14 Moreover, significant advancements in productivity are contingent upon the effective implementation and integration of decoder-only LLMs into healthcare delivery systems.

Gastroenterologists and hepatologists face the dual challenge of evaluating the robustness of LLM responses for their own decision-making—whether considering LLMs for information retrieval and clinical decision support—and for their patients, who may seek med-ical advice from LLMs before or in conjunction with specialist care. A recent review published in Nature highlights six areas where LLMs could be effectively integrated into the healthcare system: virtual nursing for chronic disease management, clinical documentation, early detection of drug adverse reactions, prediction of cancer me-tastasis, and evaluation of the social determinants of health.15 These are all relevant to digestive diseases, where physicians routinely engage in the screening, treatment and surveillance of a wide array of pathologies that could greatly benefit from LLM implementa-tion.16–19 However, it is unclear if foundational LLMs are safe enough and robust to be integrated into clinical practice without risking harming patients.14,20–25

To address the question of decoder-only LLM accuracy when used in gastroenterology and hepatology, we conducted a system-atic review of studies using decoder-only LLMs for digestive disease applications. We focus on decoder-LLM use in clinical settings and evaluate output accuracy and potential safety implications when used for diagnosis, management, and treatment.

## 2 | MATERIALS AND METHODS

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA)26 and Synthesis Without Meta-Analysis (SWiM)27 were used to guide the reporting in this study, and the checklists can be found in Table S1. The protocol was registered a priori (CRD [PROSPERO ID:42023476782]).

### 2.1 | Search strategy and selection criteria

A systematic search of the literature was conducted by a medical li-brarian (A.A.G) in the following databases: Cochrane Library, Google Scholar, Ovid Embase, Ovid MEDLINE, PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science Core Collection to identify relevant articles published from the inception of each database until January 28, 2024, using a combination of keywords and controlled vocabulary for LLMs and digestive diseases. Details of the full search strategies are listed in Table S2. The search was not limited by language, publication type or year. A second medical librarian peer reviewed the search by using the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS).28 Using Citation Chaser,29 we searched the reference lists of included studies and retrieved articles that cited the included studies with the goal of finding additional relevant studies not retrieved by the initial database search.

The search results from all databases were imported into Endnote 20, and then duplicates were removed using the Yale Reference Deduplicator.30 The deduplicated results were imported into Covidence for screening. Two independent screeners (M.G. and J.D.) performed a title and abstract review, with a third screener (D.L.S.) to resolve disagreements. The full texts of the resulting articles were then independently reviewed for inclusion by two screeners (M.G. and J.D.) and a third screener (J.H.) to resolve disagreements. For manuscripts to be considered for inclusion in this systematic review, they must have undergone peer review and meet the following cri-teria: (1) the study must query decoder-only LLMs related to the disease-related context in the field of gastroenterology and hepatol-ogy; (2) the study must use decoder-only LLMs to address questions related to screening, diagnosis, management and therapy of disease; (3) language: the article must be written in English, and questions/answers from LLMs should be provided and analysed in English; (4) the study should define the criteria and methodology used to assess the answers provided by LLMs. Manuscripts that do not meet the above criteria will be excluded from this systematic review to ensure consistency, relevance and quality in the data analysed.

### 2.2 | Outcome assessment

We focused on assessing the accuracy of LLMs, defined as the per-centage of completely correct answers (i.e., entirely accurate an-swers, fulfilling all components of the posed question without any factual or interpretational errors). The primary metric for accuracy (the most consistent outcome observed across the studies) was cal-culated as the percentage of entirely correct answers relative to the total number of answers provided. In some studies, the same ques-tion was queried multiple times, and accuracy was calculated on the overall number of responses. Therefore, there may be some discrep-ancies between the overall number of questions in the study and the overall number of answers used for accuracy calculation.

### 2.3 | Data extraction

The data extraction was executed by two independent screeners (M.G. and J.D.), and any discrepancies between the two were re-solved by consulting a third screener (J.H.). We extracted the pri-mary objectives from each study to understand the study's intent and the context in which LLMs were evaluated. We also gathered details about the specific LLMs used in the study, including their characteristics and any pertinent features. Further, we considered how the LLM prompts were engineered, refined, or manipulated for the study's purposes. Attention was given to the number of ques-tions posed to the LLM and the methodologies employed to design or acquire these questions. We also noted whether these questions were reported directly within the main text of the manuscript or if they were relegated to supplementary materials. The number of evaluators participating in each study was recorded, as was informa-tion concerning their background and expertise in the gastro/hep domain or in LLM assessment. An essential part of our extraction process was to understand the grading or assessment system em-ployed by the evaluators. This includes any scales, rubrics, or qualita-tive methods that were used to evaluate the LLM's outputs.

### 2.4 | Bias evaluation

The quality of the included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools.31 These tools evaluate the level of methodological rigour in a study and the degree to which the study has considered and accounted for potential biases in its design, implementation and analysis. According to the JBI guidelines, the evaluation was carried out by two authors (M.G. and J.H.), and disagreement was resolved by a third evaluator (K.Y.).

### 2.5 | Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Given the high variability of LLM version, prompt engineering and prompt type, we decided to descriptively report accuracies if this was part of the study design. Due to the heterogeneity of the studies with inconsistent data availability and the absence of shared out-comes, no statistical tests were performed.

#### 2.5.1 | Question representation inside the embedding space

All the available questions from the included manuscript were collected and labelled by two authors (J.D. and M.G.) by topic (gastrointestinal, pancreatic and liver disease), question type (gen-eral question vs. clinical scenario) and perspective (patient vs. physician).

Once stored and labelled, each question underwent a series of preprocessing steps to ensure uniformity and enhance the em-beddings' quality, including lowercasing, removing punctuation and special characters, and word-based tokenisation. Given all the studies investigating ChatGPT, we used the text-embedding model ‘text-embedding-ada-002’ provided by OpenAI.32 This model transforms each pre-processed sentence into a high-dimensional vector, capturing semantic and syntactic meaning. The embedding process is executed under standard parameters as defined by the model's documentation. To visualise and analyse high-dimensional embedding space, we employed t-distributed stochastic neighbour embedding (t-SNE), a machine learning algorithm designed explic-itly for dimensionality reduction of high-dimensional data.33,34 The t-SNE algorithm is applied to the sentence embeddings to project them into a two-dimensional space, facilitating the exploration of their relative distances and clusters. The parameters of the t-SNE are chosen to better represent the distance between the distribution of the reduced vectors (i.e., perplexity of 50, learning rate of 1000, number of components of 2, and random state equivalent to 84). The two-dimensional t-SNE output is analysed to identify clusters and patterns in the sentence embeddings. We assess similitude be-tween questions to understand semantic similarities or differences by visual inspection only. The entire process was conducted using Python 3.10 (libraries: scikit-learn for t-SNE and matplotlib/seaborn for visualisation).

## 3 | RESULTS

The literature search yielded 1671 results, and after the removal of duplicates, 933 citations underwent title and abstract screening (Figure 1). The exclusion of 908 citations due to a lack of relevance to the research question resulted in 33 citations for full-text re-view. The review of their full texts enabled the exclusion of 7 ci-tations, and finally, 18 studies were included.35–52 The excluded studies had unrelated tasks (n= 4, with tasks related to question generation or report summary) or unrelated settings (n= 3, ad-dressing questions not strictly related to gastroenterology and hepatology; Table S3).

### 3.1 | Study characteristics

The main study characteristics are summarised in Table 1. As re-ported in Figure 2, Out of 18 studies, 7 (38.9%) involved queries related to colonoscopy and colorectal cancer,36–38,40,49,50,52 5 (27.8%) were involved in general gastrointestinal topics (includ-ing gastroesophageal reflux disease, inflammatory bowel disease, and miscellaneous queries),35,39,42,43,51 5 (27.8%) were performed for hepatology-related topics (including liver radiology, liver transplantation, steatotic liver disease, liver cirrhosis, and hepatocel-lular carcinoma)44–48 and 1 (5.5%) focused on pancreatic disease.41

All studies used ChatGPT from OpenAI, though some studies (n= 3, 13.7%) investigated multiple LLMs, including Bard from Google36,44 and YouChat or BingChat.52 In particular, 12 (66.7%) studies reported the version of the LLM.36,38–40,42–45,47–49,51 None of the studies used fine-tuning strategies but queried LLM foundational models. Among the included studies, 2 (11.1%) reported using prompt engineering strategies,38,44 1 (5.5%) probably used a general prompt engineer-ing strategy to assess disease severity, without a straightforward task to follow43 and 1 (5.5%) study employed prompt engineering with contextualised guidelines.49 Notably, only Lim et al.49 used the OpenAI Action Programming Interface (API) to interact with the GPT models using prompt engineering and delivery of an external source of data (i.e., relevant guidelines), whereas all the other stud-ies queried ChatGPT using the online webchat interface.

### 3.2 | Question generation

For studies with available question lists, the average number of questions asked per study was 40.5 (±38.4), with a maximum of 16448 and a minimum of 837 questions, with 9 (50%) studies fail-ing to provide clear information on how the questions were gener-ated.35,36,39,41,43,44,46,47,50 In terms of question availability, 2 (14.3%) studies reported only partial lists,38,39 and 3 (21.4%) studies reported only case summaries or keywords without a clear prompt to address the aims of the study.42–44

### 3.3 | Question clustering

The application of the t-SNE algorithm to sentence embeddings resulted in a two-dimensional representation that reveals distinct clusters and dispersion patterns corresponding to questions from various studies. As depicted in the visualisation, each cluster is colour-coded according to the study it originated from, with a legend provided to identify each study's respective dataset (Figure 3A). The t-SNE plot demonstrates that ques-tions from the same study tend to aggregate, suggesting a degree of se-mantic and syntactic similarity within questions from a single study. This could indicate specific linguistic styles or terminologies employed consist-ently within individual studies. The distribution and density of the clusters could also reflect the diversity of the questions within each study. Further analysis identified clear clusters when questions were labelled according to the topic (Figure3B) or question type (Figure3C). When questions were labelled according to their main topic, 57.4% (n= 374) involved gastroin-testinal queries, 37.5% (n= 244) involved hepatology-related queries and 33 (5.1%) focused on pancreatic disease. In terms of question type, 83.4% (n= 543) were framed as general questions, whereas 16.6% (n= 108) were framed as clinical cases. On the contrary, clear clusters were not identified when questions were labelled according to patient (n= 121, 18.6%) versus physician perspective (n= 530, 81.4%) as shown in Figure 3D.

### 3.5 | Answers grading systems

The evaluation systems employed by the selected studies were heterogeneous, with findings summarised in Table 2. Numerical grading systems were employed by a significant portion of the studies (n = 7, 38.8%).35–37,42,45,47,48 Within this subset, three studies reported using a Likert Scale,37,42,45 with 2 studies em-ploying a 7-point Likert Scale37,42 and 1 study utilising a 6-point Likert Scale.45 In contrast, the remaining studies employed di-verse evaluation methodologies. These alternative approaches included scoring responses on a scale of 0–2, categorising them as entirely correct, correct but incomplete or incorrect.36 Certain studies focused on compliance with specific guidelines, categoris-ing responses as appropriate or inappropriate, while others evalu-ated specificity.38,40,44 Other studies concentrated on agreement between evaluators and ChatGPT regarding disease severity and hospitalisation recommendations.43 In the context of liver dis-ease, imaging choices were categorised using the Appropriateness Criteria, with points assigned based on recommendation appro-priateness.44,52 Finally, certain studies used qualitative grading categories like ‘Poor’, ‘Fair’, ‘Good’, ‘Very Good’ and ‘Excellent’,46 while others categorised responses as accurate, inadequate or in-accurate, and some employed grading systems based on compre-hensive, correct but inadequate, mixed incorrect and incorrect/outdated data or completely incorrect criteria.48,51

### 3.6 | Accuracy evaluation

Specific studies reported the performance of LLMs in terms of fac-tual accuracy, measured as the ratio of completely correct answers to all answers. It was possible to measure accuracy in seven studies. Among these, four studies evaluated accuracy using ChatGPT-3.5, showing accuracy of 6.4% (NOQ = 47),36 28.9% (NOQ = 69),39 45.4% (NOQ = 164)48 and 48% (NOQ = 20)47 accuracy. Three studies evalu-ated ChatGPT-4, with Kerbage et al. showing accuracy for 48% of patient-oriented questions and 40% for physician-oriented ques-tions (NOQ = 65),51 and the others showing 90% (NOQ = 20)38 and 91.4% (NOQ = 47)36 accuracy, respectively. In addition, Lim et al. evaluated ChatGPT-4 with contextualised guidelines (NOQ = 62) and showed higher overall accuracy (79% vs. 50.5% for GPT-4 without contextualised guidelines).49 For explanatory purposes, in Figure 4, it is shown the impact of contextualising guidelines using retrieval-augmented generation to direct GPT-4 Turbo outputs towards more accurate information using a question randomly selected from the study by Mukherjee et al.50 regarding colonoscopy follow-up after polyp resection.

Other studies employed diverse metrics to define output accu-racy, including numerical scales. For example, Lahat et al. reported average efficacy scores (5-point scale) of 3.3, 3.2 and 3.5 for ques-tions related to treatments, symptoms and diagnostic tests, re-spectively.35 Regarding clarity and comprehensiveness, Lahat et al. reported average clarity scores (5-point scale) ranging from 3.7 to 3.9,35 with Pugliese et al. showing an average comprehensiveness score of 2.87 (6-point scale).45 Besides, when compared to non-AI responses, Lee et al. found that ChatGPT's answers held equiva-lent values across ease of understanding, scientific adequacy and user satisfaction, with mean values ranging from 4.9 to 6.5 (7-point scale).37 Interestingly, when ChatGPT was used to make patient recommendations, it demonstrated a tendency to recommend hos-pitalisation more frequently than actual clinical practice (88.9% vs. 60%),43 suggested appropriate imaging in liver disease in 89% of cases,44 while recommending longer follow-up colonoscopy for pa-tients with high-risk polyps in 10% of cases.38

### 3.7 | Study bias evaluation

Table 3 summarises the quality assessment performed using the JBI critical appraisal tool. Four appraisal questions related to sample frame, sample size and generalisability towards general or local popu-lations do not apply to any studies. In addition, in any of the studies, it was impossible to determine if the findings were robust enough to conduct sensitivity analysis (low number of questions, low number of graders and general unclear criteria for question generation). This col-lection of critical issues places all the studies at a high risk of bias. The lack of clarity in the criteria for question generation, combined with the insufficiency in the number of questions addressed and graders involved, significantly undermines the reliability of the findings.

## 4 | DISCUSSION

Generative LLMs as medical chatbots are currently unreliable for applications to clinical questions related to gastroenterology and hepatology. We found variable accuracy ranging from 6.4% to 91.4% across different versions and prompt architecture strategies. Similar findings were found by a recent systematic review that evaluated ac-curacy across several medical specialties, reporting accuracy between 36% and 90%.21 This finding is significant because no established benchmark for LLM application to clinical tasks exists beyond per-formance on multiple-choice medical licensing examinations53; no-tably, ChatGPT failed to pass the Multiple-Choice American College of Gastroenterology Self-assessment Test.54 Our systematic review highlights the variable accuracy and lack of standardisation in report-ing metrics of LLM performance in gastroenterology and hepatology.

### 4.1 | Strengths

This is the most comprehensive, updated systematic review of ap-plications of LLMs focused on gastroenterology and hepatology and the first to our knowledge that combines quantitative and qualita-tive metrics. We performed a qualitative evaluation of the questions using stringent criteria of accuracy, allowing for some comparison across studies. Since healthcare is high stakes, the accuracy of medi-cal advice provided to patients and providers is a primary concern. Inaccurate information can lead to misinformation, posing serious patient safety risks such as incorrect treatment choices, delayed in-terventions or unnecessary medical procedures, all of which could harm patients.14 Our study highlights that the model version may impact accuracy, which is essential for future studies with LLMs. Older versions of ChatGPT showed lower rates of completely

accurate outputs. For example, accuracy ranged from 6.4% to 45.4% with ChatGPT-3.5,36,39,47,48 and was between 40% and 91.4% with ChatGPT-4.36,38,51 This is likely due to a combination of deeper neural networks, alignment with reinforcement learning with human feed-back, and access to more up-to-date information (e.g. ChatGPT-4 has real-time access to the Internet, whereas previous versions only had access to the Internet up to a specific date).55,56 We also present a quantitative cluster analysis to characterise the prompts studied across published studies. The representation within the embedding space may help in characterising the behaviour of LLM systems for specific sub-domains, such as gastroenterology and hepatology. We can visualise clusters of questions according to medical topics and between general questions versus clinical case scenarios (Figure 3C), which helps to generate hypotheses regarding the behaviour of the LLM in the embedding space given different prompts. These prelimi-nary results are a first step in quantifying the differences between LLM responses, which may help evaluate the varied linguistic de-mands of the healthcare domain.

### 4.2 | Limitations

Heterogeneity in definitions of accuracy, question content and topic, and the absence of a benchmark to contextualise find-ings limit the ability to draw conclusions across the studies and will delay the safe deployment of LLMs into clinical practice. Furthermore, the inconsistent definition of an expert and the variation in scales used for grading responses preclude any meta-analysis that could be performed. We believe that our findings of this heterogeneity in definitions across studies highlight the need for standardised evaluation metrics for both methods involving research (e.g., model-related specifications, standalone queries vs. continuous conversation or question generation techniques) and results evaluation (e.g., number, expertise and blindness of evaluators or accuracy definition), which renders all the included studies susceptible to a high degree of bias. In addition, all stud-ies are cross-sectional, with no prospective evaluation of how the responses may affect relevant outcomes for patients or provid-ers. Few studies evaluated more than one LLM, and none assessed the impact of hyperparameters (e.g., temperature thresholds). Only one study employed retrieval-augmented generation to bind ChatGPT responses to relevant guidelines, and none explored the performance of LLMs that underwent supervised fine-tuning or reinforcement learning with human feedback.

### 4.3 | Ethical considerations

The use of LLMs as medical chatbots can be complicated by vari-ous ethical implications, including handling personal health infor-mation and the liability when LLM-generated answers are used for patient care. Close-source LLMs, such as ChatGPT, do not guarantee the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) management of personal data, which could compro-mise data security and confidentiality. It is, therefore, unsafe and not advisable to use ChatGPT or similar non-compliant systems to handle sensitive health information.57 The other pivotal issue is related to liability, given the highly variable accuracy among models and settings. In traditional healthcare settings, liability often falls on healthcare professionals and institutions based on established legal frameworks and professional standards.58 However, the introduction of LLMs as intermediaries in patient care may complicate this scenario. Since LLMs like ChatGPT are developed and operated by tech companies, not healthcare pro-viders, it blurs the lines of accountability. If a patient follows in-accurate advice from an LLM and suffers harm, it is challenging to ascertain whether the liability should rest with the technology provider, the healthcare professionals who integrated the LLM into the care process or some combination of both.59 There is also the issue of consent and informed decision-making. Patients must be aware that an LLM is part of their care process and un-derstand the risks involved. This transparency is crucial for ethi-cal practice, but it also introduces questions about how patients perceive the advice from LLMs and the extent to which they trust this information in making health decisions. To mitigate liability concerns, there could be calls for regulatory oversight, stringent testing and validation of LLMs for healthcare use, clear guide-lines on their role in patient care and robust systems for tracking outcomes and errors.

### 4.4 | Future directions

From our findings, we find that current baseline models are not yet sufficiently accurate to be deployed as medical chatbots, though we acknowledge that the versions are improving at a rapid pace. Future versions should implement strategies that infuse domain-specific knowledge into these systems using techniques such as retrieval-augmented generation and fine-tuning with specialised datasets to enhance the model's ability to understand and apply medical knowl-edge accurately.60–62 These approaches will allow the production of responses that are not just based on a generalised understanding of language but are also informed by domain-relevant information. Creating common standards is critical, and validated frameworks both for reporting and quantifying the risk of bias for studies with LLMs must be implemented for future research to enhance the reliability and applicability of these systems in healthcare settings.

## BOX 1 Glossary of key terminology regarding large language models (LLMs). This glossary aims to acquaint readers with the fundamental aspects and inner workings of LLMs.

| Keyword | Definition |
|---|---|
| **General** |  |
| Artificial Neural Network | A computational model inspired by the human brain's network of neurons, consisting of interconnected layers of nodes that process input signals through adaptive connections, using backpropagation to learn patterns from data by iteratively calculating the difference between the network's output and the target outcome |
| Deep Learning | Deep learning is a subset of machine learning that utilises artificial neural networks to identify patterns and representations from data. ‘Deep’ refers to multiple layers (>1) |
| Generative Artificial Intelligence | Generative Artificial Intelligence refers to Artificial Intelligence that can produce text, images or other types of data using neural network models |
| Natural Language Processing | A field of artificial intelligence that studies the computational techniques that enable computers to understand, interpret and generate human languages |
| **Large language models: inner structure** |  |
| Large Language Model | A language model that has a high number of parameters (typically in the hundreds of billions) that demonstrates high performance in natural language processing tasks. These models are pre-trained on large corpuses of text data using self-supervised and semi-supervised methods (defined below). They are then trained using supervised fine-tuning (defined below) and aligned with human feedback (defined below) to generate useful text in response to human queries (e.g. Claude3, GPT-4 chatbots) |
| Transformer Architecture | A type of neural network that uses an attention mechanism (defined below) to identify the portions of input data that are important for understanding meaningful concepts. This architecture consists of encoders and decoders and has a variety of configurations. All transformers include a tokeniser (which converts text into tokens), a single embedding layer (which converts tokens into vector representations) and transformer layers |
| Encoder | A component of the transformer neural network designed to process input data into an embedding (defined below) with contextualised token representations |
| Decoder | A component of the transformer neural network that converts the learned data into the desired output format |
| Hidden State | The internal state of the model captures information about the sequence of inputs |
| Attention Mechanism | An algorithmic mechanism that imitates cognitive attention by computing ‘soft’ weights for every word, specifically for its embedding, within the context window |
| Autoregressive Model | This model predicts the next output (e.g. word) using all the previous outputs in the sequence |
| Bidirectional Model | This model predicts the next output by using all previous outputs in the sequence and evaluating the effect of the predicted output on all previous outputs |
| Token | The smallest unit of data processed by a language model (e.g. a single word, part of a word or a symbol) |
| Context Window | The number of tokens that the model considers when making predictions at each time |
| Embedding | A method of representing words or tokens as vectors of real numbers, capturing the semantic meaning of the data |
| Temperature | A hyperparameter that scales the logits of the output layer, influencing the entropy of the probability distribution used for sampling the next word |
| **Large language models: applications and improvements** |  |
| Prompt | An input given to a language model that triggers it to generate or predict text, often used to direct the model's output |
| Prompt-engineering | The practice of carefully designing prompts to improve the performance of language models on specific tasks |
| In-Context Learning (retrieval augmented generation) | The foundational model retrieves information from an external information dataset and binds its response generation to the relevant information contained in the context and the instruction provided by the user through prompt engineering |
| Supervised Fine-Tuning | Adapting a preexisting large language model undergoing an additional training phase on a more task-specific labelled (supervised) dataset. Fine-tuning alters existing parameters (e.g., weights and biases in the neural network architecture) |
| Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback | A training approach where a model is refined based on feedback from human evaluators to align its outputs with human preferences |
| Alignment | The process of ensuring that AI systems' behaviours align with human intentions and ethics, often in the context of artificial intelligence safety |
| Hallucination | Information that is generated probabilistically by the LLM but is incorrect, nonsensical or entirely fabricated, despite being presented by the model as factual |
