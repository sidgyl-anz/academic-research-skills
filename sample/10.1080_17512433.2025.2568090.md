### ABSTRACT

Background: Drug-drug interaction (DDI) is a global health concern affecting patient safety and treatment outcomes. Large language models (LLMs), such as ChatGPT, offer accessible alternatives; however, their effectiveness in DDI analysis remains unclear. This review evaluates the current evidence on the performance of LLM-based chatbots in identifying DDIs.

Methods: A PRISMA-compliant systematic review (PROSPERO: CRD420251020360) was conducted using PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science (studies published between 1 January 2015, and 31 March 2025). Eligible studies included those using publicly accessible LLM chatbots for DDI detection.

Results: Nine studies (2023–2025) evaluated publicly accessible LLM chatbots, including ChatGPT, Bing AI, and Google Bard, for DDI identification. Methods varied from patient-level polypharmacy screening to single-drug checks and case vignettes. Chatbot performance was inconsistent: ChatGPT identified many potential DDIs, with ChatGPT-4.0 generally identifying more potential DDIs, but with variable accuracy, while Bing AI and Google Bard were less reliable.

Conclusion: Publicly accessible LLM chatbots demonstrate variable and partial effectiveness in detect￾ing DDIs. There is a clear need to develop dedicated, freely available chatbots designed specifically for DDI identification. Future research should focus on standardizing evaluation methods and expanding access to improve medication safety in clinical practice.

### PLAIN LANGUAGE SUMMARY

Taking many medicines at once (polypharmacy) can lead to drug-drug interactions (DDIs), where one drug affects how another works, causing side effects or reducing treatment success. Detecting DDIs is important, but it often relies on costly tools or expert knowledge, which may not be readily available in all settings. This study looked at how well public AI chatbots like ChatGPT, Bing AI, and Google Bard identify DDIs. Their performance was inconsistent across different chatbots and not reliable enough for medical use. Further research is needed to comment on their safety and accuracy.

## 1. Introduction

The rapid evolution of artificial intelligence (AI) has brought trans￾formative changes to biomedical research and clinical practice [1,2]. Among the most promising innovations are large language models (LLMs), such as OpenAI’s ChatGPT, Google’s Gemini, Anthropic’s Claude, and others, which leverage vast textual data and sophisticated architectures to perform complex natural lan￾guage processing tasks [3,4]. These models have demonstrated capabilities in medical question answering [5], clinical decision support [6], and biomedical knowledge retrieval [7]. In addition, recent studies have applied natural language processing and transformer-based models in proteomics and antigen prediction, showing improved interpretation of protein sequences and enhanced prediction accuracy [8,9]. With evolving technology, one critical application domain that has recently garnered interest is drug-drug interaction (DDI) analysis [10]. This is an area pivotal to patient safety, therapeutic efficacy, and precision medicine [11].

Traditionally, DDI analysis has relied on curated databases [12], rule-based systems, and manual review of pharmacological litera￾ture. However, these methods are often time-consuming, limited in scalability, and may lag behind the rapid accumulation of new drug interaction data [13]. AI offers a novel alternative by poten￾tially automating the extraction, classification, and interpretation of DDIs from unstructured sources such as biomedical literature, clinical guidelines, and real-world reports [14,15]. Despite their promise, the capability of LLMs in DDI analysis lacks evidence synthesis. Different studies ave evaluated LLMs using diverse methodologies, interaction datasets, and evaluation metrics, lead￾ing to fragmented insights [16]. ## 2. Methods

### 2.1. Protocol registration

This systematic mapping review was registered in the PROSPERO database (Registration number: CRD420251020360). The meth￾ods and reporting of this systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta￾Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.

### 2.2. Database search

Three databases – PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science (Science Citation Index) were searched for relevant litera￾ture. We did not include any other database, like Embase, due to logistical limitations. The literature search was con￾ducted on April 3–4, 2025. The search was limited to pub￾lications from 1 January 2015, to 31 March 2025, in PubMed to capture a decade of developments in both DDI research and LLM applications.

### 2.3. Search strategy

The search strategy was tailored specifically to each data￾base’s syntax and field-specific indexing features to ensure maximal coverage. In general, keywords include two groups – DDI and LLM Chatbot, and are used together by the AND Boolean operator. The detailed search strategy is available at the digital object identifier: https://doi.org/10. 6084/m9.figshare.29389124.

### 2.4. Eligibility and screening

This review included peer-reviewed published studies that evaluated the use of publicly accessible LLM-based chatbots (free or paid) for DDI analysis. We focused on publicly acces￾sible LLMs to ensure broad applicability. Proprietary or health-system-integrated tools are relatively new, many are under development, not widely available for independent evaluation, and may not be accessible in resource-limited settings. Eligible studies involved any chatbot application used for DDI extraction from prescriptions (e.g., polyphar￾macy), classification (identification of interaction types), or prediction (detection of potential DDIs). Only studies pub￾lished in English and available in full text were considered. Studies were excluded if they did not focus specifically on DDIs. Examples include studies addressing general drug infor￾mation retrieval, adverse drug event detection, or drug-herb /food interactions. Studies employing traditional machine learning methods without LLM-based chatbots were also excluded, as were reviews or opinion articles without original analysis. Studies lacking sufficient methodological details or performance metrics relevant to DDI analysis were also left out.

Studies collected from three databases were compiled in a spreadsheet, and duplicates were removed. Abstracts were screened, and studies with an incorrect study design (e.g., review articles, letters, editorials), conference or pos￾ter presentations, or those not published in English were excluded. Full texts were then obtained for the remaining studies. Articles behind a paywall were accessed through institutional subscriptions by two authors (HM and RKG). Two authors (HM and SM) independently screened the titles and abstracts, and any discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved by consensus. Then, full-text screening was done. Two authors (HM and ID) indepen￾dently screened the full texts, and any disagreements were adjudicated by a third reviewer (SKB). During full-text screening, studies that did not report DDIs or did not utilize LLMs were excluded. Additionally, studies focused on the development or modification of LLMs for DDI ana￾lysis were also excluded.

### 2.5. Data extraction

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers (HM and SM) manually using a pre-defined Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. Extracted data included study characteristics, name, and version of a chatbot, number of patients/pre￾scriptions, number of DDI, number of DDI identified by chatbot, reference method used, brief methodology, and conclusion of the study. After data collection by individual authors, a consensus was reached with an adjudicator’s (SRV) decision to compile a final list of eligible studies to add.

### 2.6. Data synthesis

A qualitative synthesis was carried out to summarize the performance of LLMs in detecting and explaining DDIs. A descriptive evidence map was created to summarize the characteristics of the included studies, including chatbot types, reference databases, study designs, methodologies, and outcomes. Trends and gaps in the literature were illu￾strated using tables and charts. No meta-analysis or pooling of proportions was performed in order to avoid misleading conclusions due to methodological heterogeneity. This approach provides a clear overview of current research

## 2.7. Data visualization

Data visualization was performed using RStudio (version 2025.05.1; Posit Software, Boston, MA, U.S.A.) with the ggplot2 and dplyr packages. Study data, including author, chatbot type, total DDIs, and DDIs identified by each chatbot, were reshaped using pivot_longer to facilitate plotting. A horizontal grouped bar chart was generated with geom_bar(stat = ‘iden￾tity,’ position = ‘dodge’), with colors distinguishing total DDIs from identified DDIs. Plot labels, title, and legend were custo￾mized for clarity and readability.

## 3. Results

A total of 176 publications were found from databases, including 49 from PubMed, 110 from Scopus, and 17 from Web of Science. Titles and abstracts were used to screen 150 articles after 26 duplicate removal. Nine studies eventually satisfied the inclusion criteria and were included in this systematic review after 58 of them were chosen for full-text review. In Figure 1, the PRISMA flow chart is displayed.

Included studies are presented in Table 1 [10,17–24]. The review included four types of LLM chatbots -ChatGPT-4.0, ChatGPT-3.5, Bing AI, and Google Bard. There were six types of reference sources used for comparison, including MediQ, Lexicomp, Micromedex, Drugs.com, pharmacist/pharmacologist judgment, and studies where the reference source was not specified. The overall findings indicate that while AI chatbots show potential in identifying DDIs, their performance is inconsistent and generally not reliable enough for clinical decision-making. Most studies caution against using chatbot outputs as standalone references, emphasizing the need for human oversight and further improvement in accuracy and reliability before integration into healthcare practice.

## 4. Discussion

This systematic mapping review evaluated the accuracy of various LLM chatbots in identifying DDIs. The included studies assessed four different chatbots – ChatGPT-4.0, ChatGPT-3.5, Bing AI, and Google Bard. They were evaluated using various reference stan￾dards, including commercial drug interaction databases, expert opinion, and unspecified sources. While the chatbots showed potential in recognizing DDIs, their performance was inconsistent across platforms and study contexts. ChatGPT-4.0 demonstrated the highest accuracy, while Bing AI had the lowest. The consider￾able heterogeneity across studies, even within the same chatbot subgroup, suggests that factors beyond the chatbot model itself influence performance.

Aksoyalp and Erdogan found that ChatGPT-3.5 outperformed all other tools, showing strong sensitivity and specificity [19]. Bing AI performed poorly, missing most true interactions. The perfor￾mance of AI chatbots varied notably depending on the reference source. When Micromedex was used, ChatGPT models showed lower performance in comparison when Drugs.com was used as a reference [20]. Differences in reference databases can affect chatbot performance because each source may vary in coverage, classification criteria, and level of detail for DDI, leading to incon￾sistent detection across studies. Hence, the absence of standar￾dized verification methods (i.e. reference database) [25] for DDIs across studies may have contributed to the observed inconsis￾tency. In addition, the use of region-specific interaction prompts in the authors’ study may explain differences in results, as local clinicians are more familiar with commonly observed drug inter￾actions in their geographic area.

The LLM models are evolving. There are various successful applications like patient education [26], public health applications [27], and digital healthcare needs, among others [28]. However, for the purpose of DDI identification, some studies reported too low performance with only 0.243 sensitivity (specificity 0.929) [23]. However, it is important to remember that the risks of using LLMs, including hallucinations, incorrect answers, and limited ver￾ifiability, highlight the importance of human-in-the-loop oversight in clinical applications [29,30].

Hence, the potential application of chatbots is yet to be experimented. There is a need to introduce freely accessible,

dedicated chatbots specifically designed for DDI identification to support safer and more efficient medication management in clinical practice. For example, Kim et al. reported that the implementation of MEDIC enables the detection of contra￾indicated medications using only packaging information, alert￾ing users to potential adverse drug effects and supporting improved patient care in clinical environments. While MEDIC illustrates the potential of specialized tools, freely and widely available chatbots could broaden access and applicability, particularly in resource-limited settings [31]. These types of innovation should be integrated for better DDI identification in healthcare settings.

LLM chatbots have multiple dimensions that need considera￾tion while studying their capability in the prediction of DDI. Methodological factors contributing to variability in chatbot per￾formance include prompting style (e.g., structured questions ver￾sus open-ended queries) [32], subscription tier (free versus paid versions), date of model access (reflecting different model updates), and language of the input. These factors can system￾atically influence results; for example, more detailed or structured prompts generally improve DDI identification, whereas restricted subscription tiers or outdated model versions may reduce sensitivity.

In addition to the development of the new technology, it is the need of the hour to train healthcare professionals for optimal use of the tools. A randomized controlled trial by Goh et al. showed that the availability of an LLM as a diagnostic aid does not lead to a significant improvement in clinical reasoning compared to tradi￾tional resources [33]. Another study also showed that the response of the chatbot changes when it is accessed from different geogra￾phical locations [34]. Hence, along with empowering manpower, it is necessary to build chatbots for equal accessibility across the globe [35].

This study has several limitations. Major biomedical data￾bases such as Embase were not included due to logistical constraints, which may have resulted in the omission of relevant pharmacology and informatics studies. Future work should consider incorporating Embase or IEEE Xplore to minimize potential selection bias. Restricting inclusion to English-language full texts may introduce language bias. However, in this study, we found one study in Italian, and it was excluded in the title and abstract screening phase as it was related to herbal-drug interaction. The included studies varied in methodology and lacked uniform reporting stan￾dards. Many of the studies had small sample sizes or drug interactions of a particular drug. The small number of studies per chatbot type limits the generalizability. Despite these limitations, the study’s strengths lie in its systematic approach and the inclusion of multiple chatbot platforms to quantify and compare performance across models.

## 5. Expert opinion

The integration of large language model (LLM)-based chatbots into clinical practice for drug-drug interaction (DDI) screening holds potential but remains premature. The considerable variabil￾ity in performance across different chatbot platforms underscores the lack of standardization in both development and evaluation methods. While some chatbots may assist in the preliminary iden￾tification of DDIs, their inconsistent accuracy and limited validation against established pharmacological references raise concerns about their reliability in clinical decision-making. At present, these tools should be used cautiously and only as supplementary aids under the supervision of qualified healthcare professionals. Robust validation through standardized clinical testing, transpar￾ent documentation of model capabilities, and alignment with regulatory standards will be essential before such technologies can be confidently adopted in routine clinical workflows.

## 6. Conclusion

LLM-based chatbots are being explored for their potential in identifying DDIs, with some models, particularly ChatGPT-4.0, showing higher accuracy. However, performance across plat￾forms remains inconsistent and insufficient for standalone clinical use. The substantial heterogeneity observed among studies suggests that findings should be interpreted with caution. To support the safe integration of these tools into clinical practice, future research should emphasize standar￾dized evaluation protocols, consistent use of validated refer￾ence datasets, and a deeper understanding of contextual factors influencing chatbot accuracy.
