## Safety of patient-facing digital symptom checkers

Misdiagnosis by physicians occurs in 
approximately 5% of outpatients.1
Computerised diagnostic decision 
support (CDDS) programmes can help, 
and interest in this area has increased 
alongside advances in artificial 
intelligence and wider availability of 
clinical data. Originally designed for 
doctors, CDDS called symptom checkers 
are designed to directly assist patients 
by creating differential diagnoses and 
advising on the need for further care.

The health technology company 
Babylon recently claimed that their 
Babylon Diagnostic and Triage System 
outperformed the average human 
doctor on a subset of the Royal College 
of General Practitioners exam.2
 They 
supported this claim with an internal 
evaluation study,3
 the results of which 
were met with scepticism because 
of methodological concerns.4,5 In 
particular, data in the trials were 
entered by doctors, not the intended 
lay users, and no statistical significance 
testing was performed. Comparisons 
between the Babylon Diagnostic and 
Triage System and seven doctors were 
sensitive to outliers; poor performance 
of just one doctor skewed results in 
favour of the Babylon Diagnostic and 
Triage System. Qualitative assessment 
of diagnosis appropriateness made by 
three clinicians exhibited high levels of 
disagreement. Comparison to historical 
results from a study by Semigran and 
colleagues6,7 produced high scores for 
the Babylon Diagnostic and Triage 
System but was potentially biased by 
unblinded selection of a subset of 30 of 
45 test cases. The detailed analysis is 
shown in the appendix.

Babylon is commended for releasing a 
fairly detailed description of the system 
development and the three evaluation 
studies. This is an important first step 
in determining its performance and 
safety. Overall, these results suggest 
that the Babylon Diagnostic and Triage 
System potentially showed some 
improvement compared to the average 
symptom checkers in the Semigran 
study.6 However, methodological 
issues mean that any performance 
improvement is not proven.

It is not possible to determine how 
well the Babylon Diagnostic and 
Triage System would perform on a 
broader randomised set of cases or 
with data entered by patients instead 
of doctors. Babylon’s study does not 
offer convincing evidence that its 
Babylon Diagnostic and Triage System 
can perform better than doctors in 
any realistic situation, and there is 
a possibility that it might perform 
significantly worse. If this study is the 
only evidence for the performance 
of the Babylon Diagnostic and Triage 
System, then it appears to be early 
in stage 2 of the STEAD framework 
(preclinical). Further clinical evaluation 
is necessary to ensure confidence in 
patient safety.

Similar concerns with the perform￾ance of other CDDS for patients have 
been reported. Wolf and colleagues8
showed a high false negative rate 
in three of four systems designed 
to detect melanomas from images, 
which if used in the real world could 
falsely reassure patients and put 
their lives at risk. Symptom checkers 
with significant false negative rates 
could create similar dangers if used 
by patients presenting with high risk 
diseases such as cardiac ischaemia, 
pulmonary embolism, or meningitis.

These cases highlight the urgent need 
for guidelines on robust evaluation of 
CDDS directed at patients for safety, 
efficacy, effectiveness, and cost. Such 
guidelines should form the basis of 
a regulatory framework, as there is 
currently minimal regulatory oversight 
of these technologies.9
 Without such 
structure, commercial entities have 
little incentive to develop a culture that 
supports peer-reviewed independent 
evaluation.

Problem definition Informatics interventions should be problem-driven rather than technology-driven; it is essential to clearly identify the

Algorithm performance Early stage evaluations typically test systems using standard patient cases; cases should include common conditions, 
important conditions requiring urgent care, incomplete presentations, and normal patients; an independent database of 
unseen cases would provide consistency and allow direct comparisons between systems

User interaction Studies are required to ensure that users can understand a system’s purpose and limitations, and safely and effectively 
navigate the system and complete tasks, often with minimal to no formal training

Early observational studies in 
clinical settings
Early field trials should test symptom checkers in a safe, observational manner, where patients continue to receive 
standard care; this allows evaluation of real patient problems, comparison of machine and clinician recommendations, 
and analysis of data entered by patients

Formal field trials, including 
randomised controlled trials for 
impact evaluation
Pragmatic randomised controlled trials, including cluster randomisation and stepped wedge study designs, should assess 
if use of the symptom checker leads to better health outcomes than usual care; metrics could include emergency 
department visit rates, morbidity, and mortality; costs and cost-effectiveness studies are also required

System efficacy during routine 
operational use (post-market 
surveillance)
Once a symptom checker is deployed for real care, a subset of cases should be routinely and randomly audited, with 
results made public; this can ensure that algorithm performance does not decline due to algorithm or infrastructure

To ensure robust evaluation, 
we advocate publication of study 
protocols and data sets. Comparative 
effectiveness assessments also require 
clear descriptions of the underlying 
technologies used (even if commercially 
sensitive details are withheld). Although 
open publication may provide valuable 
information to competitors, it is now 
standard practice in the pharmaceutical 
industry. Critically, access to study data 
will allow for study replication, which 
remains rare in health informatics.15

Evaluation guidelines specific to 
symptom checkers have three benefits. 
First, they would provide system 
creators with a fixed set of criteria, 
ahead of time, on which they will 
be assessed. Second, they would 
allow external observers to assess 
the comprehensiveness and quality 
of evaluation, discouraging system 
creators from inflating the importance 
of their results. Finally, they would 
facilitate policy makers in determining 
a minimum level of evidence required 
before wide-scale use of a system.

fractures

We read the article by Mohit Bhandari 
and colleagues1
 (April 15, p 1519) with 
great interest. Their trial compares 
the effect of a sliding hip screw 
versus cancellous screws on the risk 
of reoperation in patients with a low￾energy hip fracture requiring fracture 
fixation. Other than what was stated 
in the title and in the summary, the 
article seems to focus only on femoral 
neck fractures and not on all hip 
fractures, which might be misleading. 
Furthermore, the authors reported 
several limitations, but we have found 
some more which must be considered.

Firstly, the age range of patients 
included in the study was not ap￾propriate for studies of patients with 
hip fractures. The general population 
of patients with hip fractures is older, 
and so including patients of at least 
50 years of age hinders generalisability 
of findings. It is widely known that 
older patients with hip fractures 
have poorer outcomes and higher 
complication rates,2,3 so the authors 
should be cautious when pooling 
younger and older patients in the 
same group. The mean age of patients 
included in the study is 72·1 years, 
which is lower than the mean age of 
patients in other studies, and creates 
a younger and subsequently healthier 
population.2,4 Furthermore, the 
reported follow-up rates of 91% are 
higher than in other similar trials2,5,6
which could possibly also be related 
to the lower mean age of the study 
population. Unfortunately the authors
