ABSTRACT
This systematic review aimed to evaluate the effectiveness, acceptability, and safety of conversational agent (CA) 
interventions in diabetes care. CAs are artificial intelligence driven tools that simulate human-like dialogue and 
have emerged as promising supports for self-management in chronic disease. We searched six electronic data￾bases from inception to June 2024 and identified 16 eligible studies involving 9076 participants across 13 
countries. Included studies varied in design, population, diabetes type, and intervention duration. Eleven studies 
assessed effectiveness, with most reporting improvements in glycemic control (e.g., HbA1c reductions of 0.3 % to 
1.0 %), medication adherence, health behaviours (e.g., diet, physical activity), or mental health outcomes (e.g, anxiety). Thirteen studies examined acceptability and found that most users had positive emotional and moti￾vational responses, though some expressed dissatisfaction with repetitive or impersonal interactions. Only four 
studies addressed safety, and while adverse events were rare, mechanisms such as clinical escalation protocols 
were inconsistently applied. Most studies were rated as weak in methodological quality, with small samples and 
limited use of control groups. In conclusion, CAs show promise as scalable, patient-centered tools for diabetes 
care. However, rigorous research is needed to better understand their clinical impact, safety, and suitability for 
diverse patient populations.
1. Introduction
In 2021, the Global Burden of Disease Study estimated that 529 
million individuals (6.1 % of the global population) were living with 
diabetes mellitus (DM), with prevalence projected to rise to 10 % by 
2050 [1]. Without effective management, DM can lead to serious com￾plications such as cardiovascular disease, neuropathy, nephropathy, and 
retinopathy. Management of DM typically includes lifestyle changes (e. 
g., diet, exercise, smoking cessation) and pharmacotherapy (e.g., met￾formin, insulin) [2]. Routine physician follow-up and structured dia￾betes education programs are known to be effective clinical strategies 
for implementing and managing treatments for DM, but can face barriers 
related to access, time, and sustained patient engagement [3,4].
Core to these strategies is diabetes self-management education 
(SME), which aim to equip individuals with the knowledge, problem￾solving skills, and confidence needed for day-to-day management [5]. 
SME typically covers blood glucose monitoring, appropriate medication 
use, healthy eating, physical activity, and recognition of hypo- or hy￾perglycemia symptoms. Its effectiveness in improving short-term gly￾cemic outcomes, such as hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), is well-established 
[3]. SME may be delivered by trained diabetes educators or accessed 
independently through printed or online self-help materials, including

healthcare providers, peers, and/or digital tools, can help sustain 
behaviour change and clinical improvements [6,7].
As health systems increasingly adopt digital innovation, new 
methods are emerging to expand the reach and impact of SME. Internet￾based programs, virtual coaching platforms, and online communities 
have shown promise, especially for people with T2D [8,9]. These tech￾nologies offer asynchronous and synchronous access to educational 
content and often include features such as self-tracking, automated 
feedback, and communication with health professionals. Yet, sustaining 
user engagement remains a persistent challenge, particularly when in￾teractions are passive or infrequent [10].
In this context, conversational agents (CAs), computer software 
systems that simulate human-like interactions via text or voice, have 
emerged as promising tools for chronic disease management, including 
diabetes [11]. CAs can provide real-time, interactive support for self￾monitoring, medication adherence, lifestyle modification, and mental 
health, such as managing diabetes-related distress and promoting 
emotional well-being. CA offering on-demand interactions that do not 
require physical visits have the potential to improve access, engage￾ment, and continuity of support in diabetes care.
Despite growing interest, questions remain about the effectiveness, 
acceptability, and safety of CA interventions in diabetes management 
[11,12]. Effectiveness refers to the impact of CAs on outcomes such as 
glycemic control, quality of life, and health behaviours. Acceptability 
relates to whether patients find the technology usable and beneficial, 
factors that influence uptake and sustained use [13]. Safety refers to 
concerns about the potential risks associated with using digital health 
technologies in the management of DM, such as incorrect medical 
advice.
To date, the evidence on CAs in diabetes care remains limited and 
mixed. While some studies have demonstrated promising outcomes in 
terms of improving diabetes-related behaviours and glycemic control 
[12], others have failed to demonstrate the effectiveness of CAs and 
highlighted challenges and complexities related to user engagement 
[14]. This review specifically focuses on patient-facing conversational 
agents, tools that simulate human-like dialogue to support self￾management education (SME), and examines not only clinical out￾comes, but also user acceptability and safety, which are critical for real￾world adoption [11]. This systematic review aims to synthesize avail￾able evidence on the effectiveness, acceptability, and safety of CA in￾terventions for people with DM to inform both clinical practice and 
future research.
2. Methods
This was a systematic review of published literature that adhered to 
the ‘Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Ana￾lyses’ (PRISMA) checklist [15], which has been included as Supple￾mentary File. The review’s protocol has been prospectively registered 
with PROSPERO CRD42024561437.
Broadly, the research question for this study was: “What evidence 
exists on the effectiveness, acceptability, and safety of CA interventions 
for people with DM?” This overarching question could be broken down 
into three parts. With regards to effectiveness, we asked: “Do CA in￾terventions improve health outcomes among people with DM relative to 
no CA intervention?” With regards to acceptability, we asked: “Do 
people with DM find the use of CA interventions acceptable?” With 
regards to safety, we asked: “Were any adverse events associated with the 
use of conversational agent interventions in people with DM?”. We 
included all types of DM; Type 1 (T1D), Type 2 (T2D), and gestational 
DM.
2.1. Search strategy
A health sciences librarian (TR) developed a core search strategy in 
MEDLINE in collaboration with the review team, then translated the 
strategy for use in Embase, APA PsycInfo, Scopus, CINAHL, and 
Cochrane CENTRAL Searches were conducted in these six databases to 
include all papers published from each database’s date of inception to 
June 24, 2024. All strategies used database-specific subject headings, 
keywords in natural language, and advanced search operators to capture 
two concepts: CAs (e.g. “bot”, “conversational agent*”, “voice agent*”, 
“artificial intelligen* assistant*”) and diabetes (“diabet*”). No study 
design, publication date, or language limits were applied. Conference 
abstracts were removed when allowed by the database. See Supple￾mentary File 1 for the complete search strategy.
2.2. Study selection
After employing the search outlined above, we uploaded the titles 
and abstracts to Covidence systematic review software, which removes 
duplicates automatically. Studies were subsequently selected via two 
phases: title and abstract screening and full text screening. In each 
phase, two independent reviewers (MS, LH, EF) applied the eligibility 
criteria (described below) to assess whether a study should be included 
in the review. Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved by 
discussion/consensus or by consultation with a third researcher (OM) if 
needed.
The eligibility criteria included a set of both inclusion and exclusion 
criteria. Studies were included if they evaluated the effect of a CA 
intervention on a health outcome (as defined in the outcomes section) in 
people with any type of DM (T1D, T2D, and gestational DM). We defined 
CA interventions as those involving a written or spoken conversation 
between a patient with DM and a computer software system.
Studies were excluded if (1) they assessed CAs that did not communicate synchronously (e.g., via email), (2) diabetes was not specifically addressed by the CA (i.e. all chronic conditions are tar￾geted), or (3) the study objective was to evaluate technical components 
of the conversational agent (automatic speech recognition, natural 
language understanding, etc.). Review papers, non-peer-reviewed pub￾lications, and publications in a language other than English were 
excluded. We did not exclude papers based on study design (i.e., 
quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-method studies were all eligible for 
inclusion) or year of publication. The results of the study selection 
process were compiled into a PRISMA flow diagram.
2.3. Outcomes
In line with these three questions, there were three outcomes we 
sought to capture in this systematic review. The first was ‘health out￾comes’ in people with DM. We defined this as physical health outcomes 
(cardiovascular risk factors, health behaviours, etc.), diabetes control 
outcomes (hemoglobin A1C, fasting glucose, hypoglycemic events, hy￾perglycemic events, etc.), mental health outcomes (depression, anxiety, 
distress, etc.), diabetes adherence outcomes (diabetes screening, medi￾cation, medical visits, etc.), and diabetes health literacy outcomes (hy￾poglycemia awareness, disease understanding, etc.).
The second was the ‘acceptability’ of CA interventions to patients 
with DM. Acceptability refers to how well patients are willing to engage 
with CAs based on factors such as perceived benefit, convenience, and 
comfort with the CA. In line with previous work on CAs, we conceptu￾alized acceptability by looking at user engagement indicators [13]. 
These indicators are measurable outcomes that are used to gauge the 
acceptability of a technology or CA to a particular population. Specific 
indicators include use metrics/adherence, working alliance (user 
engagement/bonding with the CA), usability, satisfaction, and other 
qualitative feedback [13].
The third was the ‘safety’ of CA interventions. This was defined as

2.4. Data Extraction
After studies were included in the review, we used Microsoft Excel to 
tabulate extracted data. The following data were extracted from 
included studies: first author, year of publication, study methodology, 
type and name of conversational agent, study duration, sample size, 
users’ medical and social demographics, study settings. We tabulated 
the effect of the CA intervention on health-related outcomes (i.e., 
physical health, diabetes control, mental health, and diabetes health 
literacy), the acceptability of the CA intervention (i.e. engagement in￾dicators, satisfaction, usability, and other qualitative feedback), and its 
safety (i.e. adverse events). Additionally, we extracted the characteris￾tics of the CA including the type of technology (e.g., mobile, computer, 
telephone or multimodal), dialogue management (generative, scripted, 
or hybrid), task orientation (e.g., short task-oriented conversation vs. 
long conversation without a specific task), agent embodiment/anthro￾pomorphism, conversational style, and therapeutic/coaching approach. 
Given the known heterogeneity of outcomes in this field of literature 
[12], we opted to narratively synthesize the data on effectiveness, 
acceptability, and safety rather than conducting a meta-analysis.
2.5. Quality assessment
We conducted a risk of bias assessment for the included studies. This 
assessment was conducted by two reviewers independently using the 
Effective Public Health Practice Project (EPHPP) Quality Assessment 
Tool for Quantitative Studies [16]. The EPHPP tool considers multiple 
study characteristics to determine a global rating of strong, moderate, or 
weak regarding potential risk of bias in the study. The rating is assessed 
through the following categories: selection bias, study design, con￾founders, blinding, data collection methods, withdrawal/dropouts and 
intervention integrity. Possible ratings were 1, strong; 2, moderate; or 3, 
weak. After assessing all domains, studies were allocated a global rating 
based on the number of total weak ratings they were given: 1, strong (no 
weak ratings); 2, moderate (one weak rating); or 3, weak (two or more 
weak ratings).
We did not restrict inclusion based on study design, as the field of 
conversational agents in diabetes care remains in its early stages and 
includes limited high-quality evidence. The EPHPP tool was selected 
because it allows for the assessment of both randomized and non￾randomized study designs. Importantly, studies with weaker designs, 
such as single-arm pilot or observational studies without control groups, 
were rated accordingly under the “study design” domain of the tool, 
contributing to a higher overall risk of bias rating. This approach 
ensured that methodological limitations were systematically captured 
and reflected in our assessment.
2.6. Data synthesis
We summarized studies descriptively according to study and patient 
characteristics and recorded the effects of interventions on patient 
health outcomes. Since study intervention, comparison condition and 
outcome measures were heterogeneous, we did not combine the 
extracted data (health outcomes) quantitatively; rather, we conducted a 
narrative synthesis of the results and summarized them in order to 
prevent generating misleading summary estimates.

3.2. Characteristics of study design
The included studies (n = 16) were published between 2016–2024 
and conducted in 13 different countries. Sample sizes ranged from 5 to 
8158 participants with a mean of 37. A total of 9076 individuals were 
included. Key study characteristics are summarized in Table 1.
The studies included in this review used a variety of research designs. 
Four were single-arm pilot studies, which explored feasibility and user 
experience without a comparison group [15–18]. Two studies used 
single-arm pre-post or longitudinal observational designs to evaluate 
changes over time within the same participants [19,20]. Three studies 
were randomized controlled trials, providing stronger evidence by 
comparing CA interventions to control groups [21–23], while one study 
employed a hybrid effectiveness-implementation RCT design to assess 
both outcomes and real-world integration [24]. Additionally, one study 
was a two-arm pilot trial comparing chatbot coaching to human 
coaching [14]. Two studies used mixed-methods implementation or 
usability designs to assess feasibility and user perceptions in real-world 
contexts [25,26]. One used a qualitative design [27] and another used a 
mixed-method evaluation of an RCT [28]. Lastly, one observational 
study focused on chatbot usage patterns and information-seeking 
behaviour in women with gestational diabetes [29].
3.3. Characteristics of study samples
Included studies targeted a range of populations and diabetes types: 
3 focused on individuals with T1D [15,16,27], 9 on T2D [14,17–19,23–26,28], and 1 on gestational DM [29], exclusively. 
Studies involving T1D included children [15,16], adolescents [27], and 
adults in combination with T2D populations [20–22]. Most studies 
focused on adults with T2D, including general adult populations 
[17,19,23–25,28], as well as specific subgroups such as older adults 
[26], adults with low income [14], and adults with comorbid depression 
[18]. One study examined conversational agent interventions among 
women with gestational diabetes [29].
3.4. Risk of bias within studies
Risk of bias was assessed using the Effective Public Health Practice 
Project (EPHPP). Overall, 12 studies were rated as weak (i.e., high risk of 
bias), three as moderate, and none as strong (i.e. low risk of bias). One 
study reported a qualitative inquiry of a CA (Baptista et al.); therefore, it 
was not assessed for risk of bias. A summary of the risk of bias assess￾ment is presented in supplementary file 2.

on health outcomes. These included glycemic measures (HbA1c, fasting 
blood glucose, insulin dosing), medication adherence, mental health 
(depression, anxiety, distress), and health-related quality of life 
(HRQoL).

mmol/mol) over 90 days, while Roca et al. found a reduction from 7.6 % 
(60 mmol/mol) to 7.3 % (56 mmol/mol). Krishnakumar et al. observed a 
smaller reduction of 0.3 % (~3 mmol/mol). Another study targeting 
T2D also demonstrated improved insulin adherence and faster 
achievement of glycemic control using a voice-based CA [23]. In that 
study, 11 episodes of non-severe hypoglycemia were autonomously 
managed, and fasting blood glucose levels remained below 130 mg/dL 
after 8 weeks.
Behavioural outcomes were also frequently evaluated. Roca et al. 
found that 76.9 % of participants shifted from complete non-adherence 
to adherence with medications. Nayak et al. reported higher insulin 
adherence (82.9 % vs. 50.2 %) and faster titration of insulin dosing (15 
vs. 26 days) in the CA group. In Mash et al., 87.6 % of participants re￾ported improved confidence in self-care, and 71.1 % made changes to 
diabetes management behaviours. Specifically, 76.1 % improved their 
diet, 53.6 % increased physical activity, and 43 % reported better foot 
care. One study reported a reduction in monthly medical visits from 2.0 
to 1.3 [18].
Six studies in our review included mental health outcomes 
[18,20–22,24,27]. Bruijnes et al. developed “Scotty,” a CA that provided 
156 patients with tailored support for diabetes distress using strategies 
from a book called Diabetes Burnout. The CA group showed reduced 
distress compared to controls who received only the book itself. Mac￾Neill et al. used the Wysa Mental Health Chatbot to deliver non-diabetes￾specific general CBT-based strategies for the management of anxiety and 
depression over four weeks. Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) 
scores declined from 6.75 (mild depression) to 3.75 (minimal depres￾sion). Gong et al.’s multi-featured CA (My Diabetes Coach) offered in￾tegrated support for self-management and emotional well-being. At 12 
months, Health-Related Quality of Life (HRQoL) scores improved 
significantly in the intervention arm compared with the control arm. A 
transient significant decrease in Anxiety symptoms (HADS anxiety 
score) was observed in the intervention arm relative to controls at 6 but 
not 12 months. While no change in HbA1c was observed in this study, 
significant improvements were seen in health-related quality of life and 
anxiety. Similarly, Roca et al. used a Virtual Assistant for Medication 
Adherence that provided daily reminders over nine months, which re￾ported reduction in depression scores (PHQ-9) from 13.2 to 8.6 among 
older adults with T2D and co-occurring depression.
3.6. Acceptability of the conversational agent
CAs were generally found to be highly acceptable among both ado￾lescents and adults with diabetes. In one study, an interactive humanoid 
robot assistant engaged children with T1D and received an overall 
acceptability rating of 86.7 %, with even higher acceptance among 
children aged 6–9 (94.8 %) [15]. Similarly, another humanoid robot 
named SARA achieved an overall acceptance rate of 88.2 % among 
participating children [16]. Adult participants also responded positively 
to CAs used for diabetes self-management. In Australia, approximately 
85 % of adults with type 2 diabetes (T2D) found “Laura”, an embodied 
conversational agent delivered through the My Diabetes Coach app, to be 
helpful, with 80 % of participants echoing this in a related qualitative 
study [28]. High satisfaction ratings were also reported in a U.S. study 
by Nassar et al. (2025), where 47 out of 49 participants (98 %) expressed 
satisfaction with the ease of use of the chatbot interactions. In Norway, a 
chatbot named Dina answered 2,512 out of 2,838 (~89 %) questions 
related to gestational diabetes mellitus. However, the study noted a 
slight decline in engagement—measured by the number of dia￾logues—over time [29]. Positive feedback was also reported for the 
Diabetes Education Chatbot, with users expressing satisfaction with the 
content length and frequency. However, some participants desired more 
interactive dialogue, a greater variety of questions, and deeper content 
on specific topics, summarized by one user as “Chats are a bit repetitive” 
[17]. Negative responses, though less common, were also noted. In the 
Wysa trial, two participants described the CA as “invalidating” and felt 
they were “not being heard or respected” [22]. A minority of Laura users 
characterized the chatbot as “boring,” “annoying,” or “not real,” and 20 
% reported feeling frustrated, while 17 % said it made them feel guilty 
[28]. Additional CA characteristics are included in Supplementary file 3. 
One study targeting older adults with T2D and depression (mean age 63.8 years, range 44–83) reported that nearly 40 % of participants found 
the virtual assistant difficult to use, citing challenges with menu-based 
navigation, digital literacy, and font size. Some users required tech￾nical assistance, and early dropout was linked to poor usability and 
communication difficulties with the system [18].
3.7. Safety of the conversational agent
Four out of 16 studies (25 %) assessed safety [17,23,24,27]. The 
Conversa chatbot included a clinician-facing dashboard with a traffic 
light alert system. Based on participant responses, yellow or red flags 
triggered either further chatbot engagement or direct clinician inter￾vention to address concerns such as severe hypoglycemia or anxiety 
about new medications [17]. The My Diabetes Coach program generated 
297 clinical alerts over 12 months, related to glucose levels, foot ulcers, 
and medication side effects, which were reviewed by a program coor￾dinator [24]. A voice-based CA developed by Nayak et al. autonomously 
managed hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia in patients using basal in￾sulin, providing real-time guidance without clinician input. The appli￾cation, known as Voice-Based Artificial Intelligence (VBAI), was a rules￾based, deterministic system that followed basal insulin titration algo￾rithms established by the American Association of Clinical Endocrinol￾ogists and the American College of Endocrinology. In the study, there 
were 11 episodes of nonsevere hypoglycemia, all of which were handled 
autonomously by the VBAI through appropriate insulin dose reductions. 
Notably, no adverse events occurred that required clinician intervention 
or participant withdrawal. Boggiss et al. designed the COMPASS chatbot 
for adolescents with T1D. It included an onboarding module clarifying 
that the chatbot was not a human provider and used AI to detect users in 
crisis, directing them to appropriate mental health resources.
3.8. Technical characteristics of CA
The architecture of the conversational agents across these studies 
consistently relies on rule-based systems and predefined content for 
their core dialogue generation, rather than generative AI capabilities. 
Many agents, including Motibot [20], My Diabetes Coach [28], COM￾PASS [27], the Conversational Agent Support System [21], the Diabetes 
Education Chatbot [17], t2.coach [14], and the Voice-Based Conversa￾tional AI (VBAI) [23], utilize Natural Language Understanding (NLU) or 
Natural Language Processing (NLP) to interpret user input and extract 
information like intents or self-reported data [17,20,21,23,27,28]. 
However, the subsequent conversational responses are driven by so￾phisticated script logic with predetermined variations [28], decision 
trees [27], handcrafted rules (Bassi), or deterministic algorithms for 
tasks like insulin titration [17,23]. Other examples, such as the NAO 
Robot (Majid), SARA [16], and the [22] GREAT4Diabetes chatbot [25], 
engage users through multiple-choice questions, pre-assigned educa￾tional dialogues, or sequenced audio messages requiring specific user 
replies. The virtual assistant in Roca employs Artificial Intelligence 
Markup Language (AIML) and menu-driven choices for interaction [18]. 
Even when free-text input is allowed, as in the Wysa chatbot [22] and 
Dina chatbot [29], user feedback on “repetitive” or “generic” responses 
and the presence of “fallback messages” for unanswerable queries [29] 
suggest a framework based on pre-authored content selection or tem￾plate filling rather than true generative language models.
4. Discussion
We systematically reviewed the literature to identify16 studies 
examining the use of CA interventions tailored for patients with DM. The 
studies collectively involved 9076 participants across 13 countries 
which varied in diabetes type (T1D, T2D, and gestational), age groups, 
socioeconomic contexts, and intervention durations. Of these, 11 
assessed intervention effectiveness, 13 reported on user acceptability, 
and only four addressed safety. Most studies evaluating effectiveness (9 
of 11) reported positive outcomes on glycemic control, diabetes-related 
behaviours, or mental health. However, the high risk of bias and the 
predominance of uncontrolled study design undermine the strength of 
these conclusions. CA interventions were generally well-received, with 
users reporting positive emotional and motivational responses, although 
a minority described the interactions as frustrating, repetitive, or 
impersonal. Safety features, particularly for hypo- and hyperglycemia 
and mental health crises, were embedded in only a few studies. Overall, 
this review underscores the potential utility of CAs in diabetes care given 
their high patient acceptability and emphasizes the need for more 
rigorous study designs and safety evaluation in future research.
Effective diabetes management requires sustained self-regulation of 
health behaviours, an area where self-management education (SME) 
plays a vital role. CA technologies offer a promising channel to deliver 
SME through simulated, interactive dialogue. In the studies we 
reviewed, CAs were programmed to support diabetes self-care through 
educational modules (e.g., diet, exercise, glucose monitoring, foot care) 
[17,24–26], medication adherence [18], and even automated guidance 
on insulin dosing [23]. Three out of four studies reported statistically 
significant reductions in HbA1c values ranging from − 3.3 mmol/mol or 
− 0.3% to − 10.9 mmol/mol or − 1% [17–19]. However, few studies 
defined minimal clinically important differences or linked these im￾provements to patient-centered outcomes, such as reduced complication 
rates or hospitalizations. Future research should incorporate these 
thresholds to better assess the real-world clinical significance of CA￾driven changes in glycemic control and adherence. Nonetheless, the 
observed effects on HbA1c are consistent with findings from computer￾based diabetes self-management interventions that show a small but 
statistically significant beneficial effect on blood glucose control, with a 
pooled effect on HbA1c of − 2.3 mmol/mol or − 0.2 % and a larger effect 
of − 5.5 mmol/mol or − 0.5 % in HbA1c when the interventions were 
delivered through smartphones [8]. Notably, greater user engagement 
was associated with greater improvements in glycemic control sug￾gesting a potential dose–response relationship [17,19]. Similarly, a 
decline in user engagement with the CA over time may help explain why 
a significant effect observed at 6 months was no longer evident at 12 
months [24]. This pattern is commonly reported across digital health 
interventions, where the novelty of the tool tends to diminish over time, 
leading to reduced engagement and a corresponding loss of effectiveness 
[30]. However, the current evidence base remains limited by method￾ological weaknesses. Only 25 % of studies employed a controlled design, 
one-third had sample sizes under 20 participants, and the majority (12 
out of 15) were rated as weak in quality, with only three studies rated as 
moderate. These findings highlight both the early stage of research on 
CA for diabetes and the high risk of bias that tempers current conclu￾sions. Although initial results are promising and suggest the potential of 
CA as scalable tools for self-management support, future research should 
adopt more rigorous scientific standards such as larger controlled trials 
with longer follow-up periods in order to assess CA’s effectiveness and 
long-term impact.
The psychological burden of diabetes is significant and often under￾addressed in routine care [31]. Emotional distress, depression, and 
anxiety are prevalent in people with DM and are known to impair self￾management and glycemic control [32,33]. CAs represent a novel 
mechanism for delivering scalable mental health support, either as a 
standalone intervention or integrated into the diabetes care pathways. 
Several of the reviewed interventions targeted both physical and mental 
health outcomes; for example, one study aimed to simultaneously 
improve glycemic control and emotional well-being [24], whereas 
others focused solely on mental health support for people with diabetes 
[18,20–22,27].
Nearly all the CA were tailored for people with diabetes, with the 
exception of one [22], which offered a generic mental health chatbot 
(Wysa) to provide cognitive behavioral strategies that target anxiety and 
depression symptoms. Notably, two of the mental health–oriented CAs 
were customized for specific subgroups; older adults with T2D and 
depression [18] and young people with T1D [27]. These examples 
highlight the flexibility of CAs as highly adaptable tools that can be 
tailored to meet the diverse needs of patients based on age and comor￾bidities. As suggested by scholars, digital health tools offer the potential 
for “precision medicine at scale” by adapting interventions to individual 
patient characteristics [34]. These findings align with trends observed in 
other chronic conditions, such as congestive heart failure, where CAs 
have been used to provide both condition-specific guidance and mental 
health support [35,36]. Because mental health care is traditionally 
delivered by clinicians, the use of CAs in this domain represents a 
paradigm shift in chronic disease management and a promising direc￾tion for future research. However, most of the CAs reviewed employed 
rule-based dialogue systems rather than AI-based generative architec￾tures. This aligns with a systematic review on CA in chronic disease 
management [11] which found that studies in healthcare primarily used 
finite-state or scripted dialogue management and did not incorporate 
real-time generative interactions (Kurniawan). This technical limitation 
may contribute to user-reported dissatisfaction with generic or repeti￾tive responses and underscores the need for more advanced generating 
novel, free-form conversational dialogue systems in future development. 
Future CAs should leverage advancements in digital empathy and 
interoperability with wearables to deliver more personalized, context￾aware interactions that enhance user engagement and long-term rele￾vance.[37,38].
To be effective, CA interventions must be well-received by patients; 
without sufficient acceptability, adoption and long-term use are unlikely 
[13]. In 13 of the 16 reviewed studies, patient acceptability was assessed 
and generally found to be high. Users commonly described positive 
experiences, citing the tools as helpful, supportive, and engaging 
[20,28]. These findings align with broader trends in digital health, 
where personalization, tone, and perceived empathy often influence 
user satisfaction [39,40].
However, negative user experiences, though less frequently reported, 
highlight important areas for improvement. Some users found the CA 
interactions impersonal, invalidating, or emotionally frustrating, espe￾cially those with more complex psychological needs [22,28]. These re￾sponses underscore that while CAs can provide meaningful support for 
many, they may not be suitable for all users or all clinical contexts. 
Similarly, older adults may face unique challenges when interacting 
with CA systems, including discomfort with numeric menus, difficulty 
understanding or being understood by the agent, and lower digital lit￾eracy. To address this challenge, researchers strongly recommend col￾lecting user feedback, particularly negative feedback, as it plays a 
critical role in refining digital technologies to better align with diverse 
patient needs and preferences [41]. For older adults, age-friendly design 
elements such as simplified interfaces, larger text, and customizable 
support features are essential to ensure equitable access and sustained 
use among geriatric populations. Incorporating user feedback is essen￾tial not only for improving functionality but also for improving patient 
acceptability. As such, user input should be an integral part of future 
development efforts.
Patient-facing digital tools must not only demonstrate effectiveness 
but also ensure safety. Yet, safety remains an under-evaluated aspect in 
the current literature on CA; only a minority of studies in our review 
explicitly addressed how the CA system addressed safety concerns. 
While some incorporated clinician dashboards or automated alert sys￾tems for issues like hypoglycemia [24] or psychological distress [27], 
these features are not yet standard, particularly among CAs that make 
treatment recommendations without clinician oversight [23].
This limitation is not unique to CAs. Earlier computer-based diabetes 
self-management tools, also lacked robust safety evaluations [8]. How￾ever, the growing use of AI in CAs increases the complexity and potential 
risk, particularly when clinical advice is generated. In contrast, rule￾based deterministic chatbots, such as those following basal insulin 
titration algorithms established by the American Association of Clinical 
Endocrinologists and the American College of Endocrinology may pose 
lower risk due to their predefined algorithms and indeed, did not yield 
any patient adverse events in the study reviewed [23]. With generative 
CAs, however, the consequences of incorrect or inappropriate guidance 
could be clinically significant [42]. Therefore, future research should 
not only evaluate the effectiveness of CAs but also systematically assess 
safety mechanisms and clearly define the role of clinician oversight in 
their deployment [34]. To enhance long-term safety and engagement, 
future CA platforms should integrate real-time user feedback moni￾toring, dropout detection, and automated escalation protocols linked to 
clinician review.
One of the major strengths of CA interventions is their adaptability. 
Several of the studies in this review tailored their tools to meet the needs 
of specific populations. Two studies focused on children and adolescents 
aged 5–16 years with T1D, using social robots to deliver diabetes edu￾cation in clinic settings [15,16]. These humanoid robots engaged chil￾dren in conversations about glucose monitoring, physical activity, and 
healthy eating. The interventions were generally well-received, partic￾ularly among younger children, who perceived the robot as a compan￾ion, suggesting age-related differences in engagement and perceived 
usefulness.
Mitchell et al. developed a two-week CA intervention targeting 
adults with T2D living in low-income communities. The chatbot guided 
participants through a brief Action Planning process to support their 
diabetes management goals. Low socioeconomic status is a known risk 
factor for poor glycemic control and diabetes-related complications 
partly due to limited access to healthcare and fewer opportunities for 
healthy behaviours, as such, CA interventions may offer a scalable, cost￾effective means of support for underserved populations [41]. Finally, 
Sagstad et al. designed “Dina”, a CA tailored for pregnant women with 
gestational DM. Dina provided education on healthy eating, safe phys￾ical activity during pregnancy, and key information specific to gesta￾tional DM. These examples illustrate the flexibility of CA technology to 
adapt content, tone, and delivery method to meet the unique needs of 
different demographic groups, making them a promising avenue for 
equitable, personalized diabetes care [34].
5. Strengths and limitations
Strengths of this review include its comprehensive search across 
multiple databases and examination of multiple CA outcome domains; 
effectiveness, acceptability, and safety. The review also highlights how 
CA interventions have been adapted for specific populations However, 
limitations include the overall low methodological quality of the 
included studies; small sample sizes and limited use of controlled de￾signs. These factors increase the risk of bias and reduce the certainty of 
the evidence since it makes it difficult to attribute observed effects to the 
CA interventions with confidence. Moreover, the lack of safety reporting 
hinders the evaluation of potential risks, an especially important 
consideration for digital tools that influence clinical decision-making. As 
most reviewed interventions were early-stage prototypes or pilot pro￾grams, implementation strategies including integration with clinical 
workflows, provider training, and use of implementation science 
frameworks were not addressed but will be essential considerations once 
CA technologies mature.
6. Conclusions
This systematic review highlights the promising role of CA in dia￾betes care, particularly in supporting self-management and mental 
health. The majority of reviewed studies suggest benefits in glycemic 
control and user engagement although the evidence base remains 
limited by small samples, short durations, and methodological limita￾tions. Acceptability is generally high, yet emotional nuance and safety 
mechanisms require further work.
CAs represent a scalable, flexible, and potentially transformative 
approach to supporting people with DM. Future research should prior￾itize randomized controlled trials, rigorously assess safety, and explore 
integration of the CA into broader diabetes care pathways. CA in￾terventions could play a critical role in expanding access to personalized 
diabetes care worldwide through the use of thoughtful patient-centered 
design and evaluation.
