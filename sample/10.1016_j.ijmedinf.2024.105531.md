ABSTRACT 
Background: PRISMA-based literature reviews require meticulous scrutiny of extensive textual data by multiple 
reviewers, which is associated with considerable human effort. 
Objective: To evaluate feasibility and reliability of using GPT-4 API as a complementary reviewer in systematic 
literature reviews based on the PRISMA framework. 
Methodology: A systematic literature review on the role of natural language processing and Large Language 
Models (LLMs) in automatic patient-trial matching was conducted using human reviewers and an AI-based 
reviewer (GPT-4 API). A RAG methodology with LangChain integration was used to process full-text articles. 
Agreement levels between two human reviewers and GPT-4 API for abstract screening and between a single 
reviewer and GPT-4 API for full-text parameter extraction were evaluated. 
Results: An almost perfect GPT–human reviewer agreement in the abstract screening process (Cohen’s kappa >
0.9) and a lower agreement in the full-text parameter extraction were observed. 
Conclusion: As GPT-4 has performed on a par with human reviewers in abstract screening, we conclude that GPT￾4 has an exciting potential of being used as a main screening tool for systematic literature reviews, replacing at 
least one of the human reviewers. 

## 1. Introduction 

PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and 
Meta-Analyses) is an evidence-based set of guidelines meant to help 
authors to conduct and report systematic reviews [1–4]. The PRISMA 
guidelines provide detailed recommendations for each part of the sys￾tematic review process and require meticulous scrutiny of extensive 
textual data by multiple reviewers [5–10], which is associated with 
considerable human effort [11,12]. 

However, owing to recent advances in natural language processing, 
specifically GPT-4 API [13–15], the review process can be considerably 
streamlined by incorporating an AI-based reviewer. Accordingly, there 
emerges the need to evaluate the degree to which GPT-4 API can facil￾itate the reviewing process. 

### 1.1. What is already known 

As of early 2024, there is already some promising work on incor￾porating GPT in the title and abstract screening of research papers for 
the purpose of a systematic literature review [16,17]. For instance, in a 
2024 publication, Guo et al. [16] evaluated whether the OpenAI 
generative pre-trained transformer (GPT) and GPT-4 application pro￾gramming interfaces (APIs) can identify relevant titles and abstracts 
from clinical review datasets; the performance of GPT models was then 
compared against that of two human reviewers. The results showed that, 
along with identifying relevant titles and abstracts, the GPT models 
provided reasoning for their decisions and amended initial decisions 
upon incorrect classifications, suggesting that models such as GPT-4 can 
enhance efficiency and lead to more accurate and reliable conclusions in 
medical research.

## 2. Methodology 

### 2.1. Systematic literature review initiation 

As the topic of a literature review where the performance of an AI￾based reviewer would be assessed against that of human reviewers, 
we chose the role of natural language processing and LLMs in facilitating 
automatic patient-trial matching within clinical trials. On September 13, 
2023, relevant studies were retrieved from Google Scholar using the 
query “patient trial matching”. Using a Python [22] script, we extracted 
the initial 1,000 search results, including the titles, partial abstracts, and 
corresponding hyperlinks. Next, full abstracts and full texts of the arti￾cles were evaluated both manually and using GPT-4 API. 

### 2.2. Manual screening of full abstracts 

Full abstracts of the search results from Google Scholar were 
manually collected and submitted to the manual PRISMA review 
screening process (see Fig. 1). Two independent reviewers (the first two 
authors, AL and DA) meticulously examined the abstracts to identify 
whether each of the articles used: 
1. Machine Learning or AI (Artificial Intelligence), 
2. Natural Language Processing (NLP) techniques. 

To minimize information retrieval fatigue, a known challenge in 
systematic reviews [23–26], we employed a dynamic predefined 
threshold strategy known as “screening saturation” [8,9,27] which 
determined when to conclude the screening process. This threshold was 
met upon identifying less than 5 % positive documents within the last 50 
articles screened (see Fig. 2). 

Subsequently, 102 articles were reviewed, out of which 2 were 
excluded because they did not have abstracts. 
Of the remaining 100 articles, 76 were excluded as they did not meet 
either of the two inclusion criteria (i.e., use of machine learning/AI and 
NLP). One more article was excluded as a duplicate. Therefore, 23 
studies were included in the final dataset. 

### 2.3. Integration of GPT-4 API into the abstract screening process 

For the comparative analysis of the AI-based screening with that 
derived by human reviewers, the abstract screening process was con￾ducted using ChatGPT-4 API. For this GPT-4 API-based extraction, our 
Python code (https://github.com/assaflandschaft/manuscript_prisma) 
using LangChain [28] was used. 

### 2.4. Inter-rater agreement measurement 

Agreement levels among the three reviewers (two human reviewers 
and GPT-4 API) and between the final set agreed upon by human re￾viewers and the data extracted by GPT-4 API were evaluated using 
Cohen’s kappa [29,30]. 

### 2.5. Manual extraction of data from full-text articles 

The data extracted from full-text articles were manually evaluated 
based on the following six parameters: (1) classification as a review or 
meta-analysis, (2) identification of the involvement of LLMs within the 
described system, (3) specification of the particular LLM architectures 
used, (4) assessment of additional structured data integration within the 
patient-trial system, (5) compilation of unique medical conditions 
referenced in clinical trials, and (6) evaluation of the patient-trial 
matching system’s impact on subject diversity. The first author manu￾ally extracted the data.

### 2.6. GPT-4 API-based data extraction phase 

In extracting data from selected full-text articles using GPT-4 API, an 
important challenge was related to token limitations inherent in GPT-4 
API. In language models like GPT-4 API, tokens represent individual 
units, thereby frequently equating to words or sub-words. Typically, an 
English word encompasses around 1.3 tokens, factoring in variations in 
word complexity and length [31]. However, the finite token limit of 
GPT-4 API, including the prompt and its various components, is set at 
8,192. This restriction poses a significant obstacle when handling 
lengthy scientific publications and requires strategic maneuvers to distill 
critical information during the data extraction phase. In the next section, 
we describe the data extraction technique that was used. 

### 2.7. Data extraction technique: RAG method and LangChain integration 

Our methodical data extraction, which integrated RAG methodology 
[32–35] and LangChain, consisted of the following four distinct steps 
(see Fig. 3; see also https://github.com/assaflandschaft/manuscript_pri 
sma). Step 1 involved loading documents via LangChain’s document 
loaders, ensuring seamless access from PDFs or text columns. 
Next, Step 2 encompassed chunking and embedding via Recursive 
TextCharacterSplitter [36], segmenting articles into contextually 
meaningful chunks (1,200 characters in length with an overlap of 400 
characters between the chunks) and converting them into vector em￾beddings for more nuanced contextual representations. 
Furthermore, in Step 3, we used the multi-query retriever technique. 
Multiple questions were used to strategically retrieve relevant text from

implementation, we decomposed each of the main questions into three 
sub-questions using our LLM. A total of 10 relevant chunks were 
retrieved for each question and the three sub-questions. The reason 
underlying our decomposing the main questions into three sub￾questions was that our experiments showed that generating three sub￾questions resulted in retrieving all chunks necessary to answer the 
main question. The full list of questions generated and the main question 
is available in the notebook “fulltext_parameter_extraction” in our 
Github repository (https://github.com/assaflandschaft/manuscript_pri 
sma). Therefore, the resulting Full Context comprised a maximum of 
40 text chunks, although the actual count might have been lower due to 
duplicates. 

Finally, Step 4 involved generating answers to our questions. Here, 
we combined several crucial components. First, the Full Context 
retrieved via the multi-query retriever in Step 3 provided the relevant 
contextual backdrop. Next, to minimize the risk of unwarranted elabo￾ration or fabricated answers and to ensure the quality of the output, we 
incorporated the Prompt [38–42], a textual guide directing our LLM to 
deliver concise and accurate responses. Next, we integrated our primary 
Question, distinct from the sub-questions, serving as the focal inquiry. 
Additionally, we defined the Output Schema, specifying the desired 
structure of the LLM output to be in the JSON format, which is a com￾mon practice in the field [43,44]. Our final input to the LLM comprised 
four components (i.e., Full Context, Prompt, Question, and Output 
Schema) combined into a textual format recognizable by the LLM. This 
amalgamation directed the LLM’s response generation. Finally, we 
conducted Question Answering for all queries and archived the re￾sponses using an Excel file as our storage repository. 

### 2.8. Calculation of inter-rater agreement for varied parameter types 

Subsequent measurement of inter-rater agreement, assessed via 
Cohen’s kappa [45], included comparisons between the human re￾viewers, between GPT-4 and each human reviewer, and between GPT-4 
and the established consensus between the two human reviewers. The 
process of calculating inter-rater agreement involved distinct method￾ologies based on the parameter types. For Boolean parameters (Param￾eters 1, 2, and 4), represented as YES/NO values, determining Cohen’s 
kappa was straightforward. Parameters 3 and 5, which included lists of 
tokens such as LLM names and disease references, required a distinct 
approach. Specifically, using one-hot encoding provided lists of entities 
that were present or not (YES/NO) and Cohen’s kappa was used nor￾mally. This approach was used in several previous studies [46–48]. To 
address the low sample size, the jackknife estimator for the variance of 
kappa was used [49]. For Parameter 6, which evaluated patient-trial 
system impact on subject diversity, a quantitative assessment via 
Cohen’s kappa calculation was not feasible because of the lack of 
measurable diversity references in the reviewed papers. 

### 2.9. Additional assessment metrics 

We settled on Cohen’s kappa as the main statistical measure because 
it accounts for chance agreement and accepts that even human raters 
make educated guesses in ambiguous cases. Previous work that inte￾grated GPT in title and abstract screening [16,17] reports measures such 
as accuracy, sensitivity, specificity and F1 score of a GPT model relative 
to the ground truth represented by human reviewer consensus. For 
comparison’s sake, we choose to report the same metrics across Boolean 
parameters. 

## 3. Results 

### 3.1. Inter-rater agreement in abstract screening 

In the screening process, two independent reviewers and our GPT 
system extracted two binary parameters (use of AI/ML and NLP in the 
described system) from 100 abstracts. The results concerning inter-rater 
agreement between the two human reviewers, GPT and the reviewers, 
and between GPT and the final values of the human reviewers’ post 
conflict resolution are summarized in Table 1. Inter-rater agreement was 
high for both parameters (AI/Ml and NLP) and in all three pairs of raters, 
with all Cohen’s kappa values exceeding 0.890. 

### 3.2. Inter-rater agreement in full-text review 

The results on 23 full-text articles [50–72] included in the final 
dataset, as along with the corresponding agreement values, are sum￾marized in Table 3. Overall, the agreement in the full-text parameter 
extraction was poorer than in abstract screening. Among the three 
Boolean parameters considered, “structured data” and “LLM use” 
showed the highest GPT-consensus agreement (Cohen’s kappa 0.65 and 
0.59, respectively), while “Review” was an edge case (Cohen’s kappa 0), 
because GPT-4 answered NO for all the reviewed articles. The consensus 
was that only one of the 23 full-text articles was a review, so GPT still 
extracted mostly accurate information. 

Regarding two list-of-tokens parameters, for “LLM name”, the 
agreement between GPT-reviewer and GPT-consensus was low (Cohen’s 
kappa − 0.18 and − 0.16, respectively). Of note, agreement was calcu￾lated based only on those articles that were identified to be using LLMs 
(n = 7). Conflicts mostly arose when either GPT or the reviewer did not 
identify the use of an LLM or mistakenly identified an LLM that was 
mentioned in the article but was not in fact part of the proposed system. 

### 3.3. Additional assessment metrics 

Accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1 score were calculated 
separately for the screening and full-text review phases (see Table 2). All 
metrics were consistently better for the abstract screening than for the 
full text review.

## 4. Discussion 

### 4.1. 4.1 abstract screening efficiency 

The first reason underlying the high inter-rater agreement observed 
in the abstract screening was that the extracted parameters were Bool￾eans, so agreement was clearly defined. Second, as the abstract texts are 
typically short, they were submitted to GPT-4 API in full, i.e. without 
splitting with the RAG-LangChain methodology. Thirdly and finally, 
abstracts typically provide a concise summary of the original work and 
contain no descriptions of other projects or future plans, which enhances 
efficiency of abstract screening with GPT-4 API. 

### 4.2. Full-text review efficiency 

The reasons underlying a lower agreement in the full-text parameter 
extraction were as follows. For Boolean parameters, conflicts frequently 
arose when the answer was not explicitly stated or was easy to misin￾terpret. For instance, in some articles, the authors would not clearly state 
whether the input data were structured or unstructured, so even human 
reviewers had to make an educated guess. It is less dependable to uphold 
answers that involve educated guesses as a gold standard, yet our full￾text review values for accuracy, sensitivity, specificity and F1 score 
were on a par with those reported in previous work that used existing 
title and abstract datasets from public systematic reviews with estab￾lished ground truth values (0.870, 0.714, 0.938 and 0.769 in this study 
vs. 0.907, 0.764, 0.910 and 0.600 in Guo et al [16] and 0.27–0.82, 
0.38–0.69, 0.25–0.85 and 0.08–0.24 in Gargari et al [17]). Importantly, 
our values for the abstract screening phase were higher than those re￾ported by Guo et al. [16] and Gargari et al. [17]; however, we only 
screened for two parameters and had a smaller dataset. It would be 
interesting to see whether our system would perform better on a larger 
data set with more exigent eligibility criteria (more parameters). As per 
inter-rater agreement, only Guo et al. [16] reported a Cohen’s Kappa 
value of 0.22, which is significantly lower than the values we observed 
for Boolean parameters (except for the review parameter, which was an 
edge case). Another common reason for conflicts was presenting infor￾mation as a counterargument or for comparison, which GPT-4 API 
mistakenly assumed to be part of the proposed system. 

For the list-of-tokens parameters, another challenge was extracting 
and resolving the list of medical conditions addressed in the trials. 
Overall, key issues encountered at this stage can be summarized as 
follows: 

Listings of the full name and/or abbreviations of the disease – e.g., 
GPT-4 API extracting “Alzheimer’s Disease (AD)” and “AD” vs the 
human reviewer extracting “Alzheimer’s Disease”. 
Different listings of the same disease – e.g., “Diabetes” vs. “Diabetes 
Mellitus” vs. “Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus”. 
Listings of medical terms that were not diseases/medical conditions – 
e.g., “Type 1”, “colorectal”. 
Listings of diseases that appeared in Related Work sections but were 
not targeted in the reviewed clinical trials. 

The challenges we encountered extracting relevant medical condi￾tions highlight the need to adapt the workflow of a GPT-based literature 
review system for domain-specific jargon. This could be achieved by 
repeated and meticulous prompt engineering, using an LLM trained on 
domain-specific data instead of GPT-4 or doing post-extraction pro￾cessing with a system that has domain-specific knowledge. Overall, the 
more complex answer one wants from GPT-4 API, such as a list of tokens, 
the more effort would have to be invested into adapting the system to 
make it domain-specific.

### 4.3. Limitations 

The present study has several limitations. First, it is difficult for two 
reviewers to read a PRISMA-based literature and list the same medical 
condition (or necessary token for GPT-4). Accordingly, extracting a list 
of tokens is challenging, as two reviewers may struggle to consistently 
identify the exact same tokens. Apart from the method proposed in the 
present study, an alternative way to enhance answer accuracy is 
employing the human reviewer’s listed tokens to generate binary￾answer questions prior to querying GPT-4. 
Second, in contrast to several previous studies that incorporated 
results from multiple survey studies, in the present work, we evaluated 
the proposed tool with a single survey task. This may have compromised 
generalizability of our findings, as well as constrained the overall impact 
of our work and the robustness of error analysis. Accordingly, in future 
research, it would be meaningful to apply our approach in several tasks. 
Third, in this study, we did not explore the reasoning ability of the 
GPT-4 model that could help perform error analysis on the full-text 
extraction results. Accordingly, in future research, it would be mean￾ingful to investigate the GPT-4 reasoning mechanisms for enhanced 
error analysis on the full-text extraction results. 

## 5. Conclusion 

With an almost perfect inter-rater agreement in the abstract 
screening process and a lower agreement in the full-text parameter 
extraction, GPT-4 API has performed on par with the human reviewers. 
These findings suggest that GPT-4 API has an exciting potential to be 
used as a main screening tool for systematic literature reviews, replacing 
at least one of the human reviewers.

What was already known on the topic? 
• GPT-based models were successfully used in the title and abstract 
screening processes for the purpose of systematic literature reviews. 
• GPT-4 can enhance efficiency and lead to more accurate and reliable 
conclusions in medical research. 

What has this study added to our knowledge? 
• We propose a workflow that employs the RAG methodology and 
LangChain to integrate a GPT-4 based reviewer in both abstract 
screening and full-text extraction. 
• We find an almost perfect GPT–human reviewer agreement in ab￾stract screening and a lower agreement in the full-text extraction.
