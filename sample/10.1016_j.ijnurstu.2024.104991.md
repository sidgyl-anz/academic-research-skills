ABSTRACT  
Background: Effective management of physical and psychological symptoms is a critical component of compre￾hensive care for both chronic disease patients and apparently healthy individuals experiencing episodic symp￾toms. Conversational agents, which are dialog systems capable of understanding and generating human 
language, have emerged as a potential tool to enhance symptom management through interactive support.  
Objective: To examine the characteristics and effectiveness of conversational agent-delivered interventions re￾ported in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in the management of both physical and psychological symptoms.  
Design: A systematic review.  
Methods: A comprehensive search was performed in Pubmed, ACM Digital Library, CINAHL, EMBASE, PyscInfo, 
Web of Science, Scopus and gray literature sources from their inception to Oct 2024. Search terms included 
“conversational agent”, “symptom”, “randomized controlled trial” and their synonyms and hyponyms. Duplicates 
were identified by EndNote, and titles, abstracts and full texts were independently screened according to pre￾defined criteria. Data extraction focused on basic study characteristics and conversational agent details, with The 
Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool employed for bias assessment.  
Results: The search yielded 2756 articles and 29 were finally included for review. The included studies pre￾dominantly came from developed countries (n = 23) and were conducted between 2020 and 2024 (n = 24). The 
studies frequently evaluated the feasibility and acceptability of conversational agent interventions (n = 14), with 
a predominantly focus on psychological symptoms (depression, anxiety, etc.) (n = 17). A few studies focused on 
physical symptoms (pain, etc.) (n = 4), while others addressed both symptoms (n = 8). Twenty-five distinct 
conversational agents (Woebot, Tess, etc.) were evaluated, utilizing platforms ranging from proprietary appli￾cations to common messaging channels like WeChat and Facebook Messenger. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy 
(CBT) was a commonly integrated approach (n = 22), with rule-based dialogs (n = 22) as the most commonly 
dialog system methods and Natural Language Processing (NLP) (n = 15) as the predominant AI techniques. The 
median recruitment and completion rates were 72 % and 79 %, respectively. The majority of studies reported 
positive user experiences and significant symptom management improvements (n = 22). However, risk of bias 
was high in seventeen studies and presented some concerns in nine others.  
Conclusions: Conversational agents have shown promise in enhancing both physical and psychological symptom 
management through positive user experiences and effectiveness. However, the high risk of bias identified in 
many studies warrants caution in interpreting these findings. Future research should prioritize the methodo￾logical quality of RCTs to strengthen the evidence base supporting the use of conversational agents as a com￾plementary tool in symptom management.

## 1. Background
Symptoms, defined as “subjective experience reflecting changes in 
the biopsychosocial functioning, sensations or cognition of an individ￾ual” (The University of California, 1994), are prevalent in chronic dis￾eases and cancer-related illnesses, such as pain, fatigue, and depression 
(Brown and Kroenke, 2009; Brugnoli, 2016). These symptoms exacer￾bate the physiological burden of the underlying disease and negatively 
impact the quality of life, treatment adherence, and prognostic out￾comes (Jimmy and Jose, 2011; Megari, 2013). Furthermore, even 
healthy individuals may experience sporadic physical and psychological 
symptoms, such as stress-induced headaches or situational anxiety, 
which can impair their well-being and productivity (Song et al., 2016). 
Symptom management, a fundamental aspect of healthcare, is defined 
as a dynamic and multidimensional process in which individuals 
intentionally and actively engage in health-promoting behaviors, 
participate in their care, and monitor their symptoms to achieve optimal 
symptom relief and improve quality of life (Dodd et al., 2001). Effective 
symptom management is essential to optimize the outcomes of in￾dividuals experiencing symptoms by relieving symptoms, enhancing 
daily functioning, improving quality of life, decreasing symptom￾associated expenses, and ultimately enhancing the efficiency and cost￾effectiveness of healthcare systems by reducing healthcare resource 
utilization (Nayak and George, 2023).

Traditional symptom management approaches often rely on face-to￾face patient-healthcare provider consultations to develop and imple￾ment symptom relief strategies and interventions (Grady and Gough, 
2014). However, these consultations often face numerous challenges 
that hinder their effectiveness. Face-to-face consultations can be limited 
by the accessibility to healthcare services and a shortage of healthcare 
professionals, leading to delayed or insufficient symptom relief (Nielsen 
et al., 2017). Moreover, regular in-person follow-ups can be challenging 
for patients with mobility issues or health conditions that restrict their 
ability to travel frequently and may place significant time and financial 
burdens on them (Ambroggi et al., 2015; Azzani et al., 2015; Paul et al., 
2016). Furthermore, some individuals may feel uncomfortable discus￾sing personal symptoms in a healthcare setting, leading to under￾reporting or avoidance of necessary care (Clement et al., 2015). Web￾based and mobile-based symptom management approaches have 
emerged as alternatives to traditional methods, offering increased 
accessibility and convenience (Edge et al., 2023; Fridriksdottir et al., 
2018). However, these interventions often provide generic information 
and support, which may not adequately address the unique needs and 
preferences of individuals (Reiners et al., 2019). Consequently, there 
remains a need for innovative symptom management strategies that can 
provide personalized, accessible, and convenient support to individuals. 
With the rapid advancement of modern technology, conversational 
agents have emerged as a promising approach to address this need in 
symptom management (Laranjo et al., 2018).

Conversational agents, commonly known as “chatbots”, are dialog 
systems capable of understanding and generating human language, 
enabling effective human-computer interactions (Allouch et al., 2021). 
The operational principle of conversational agents relies on their un￾derlying technological architecture, which can be categorized into three 
main approaches: rule-based, retrieval-based, and generative-based 
approaches. Rule-based technologies generate responses based on pre￾defined rules (Adamopoulou and Moussiades, 2020). Retrieval-based 
approaches generate responses by searching for the most relevant 
reply from a predefined database based on the similarity between the 
current user input and the stored dialog records (Adamopoulou and 
Moussiades, 2020). Generative-based technologies employ deep 
learning models trained on large datasets of human conversations to 
create novel and context-aware responses from user input (Pandey and 
Sharma, 2023). Conversational agents can be designed to provide 
personalized, accessible, and cost-effective support. By engaging in 
interactive dialogs and dynamically learning from user responses, these 
systems can dynamically tailor their support to a wide range of users, 
providing a personalized management experience (Kocaballi et al., 
2019). Moreover, conversational agents enable individuals to engage in 
dialogs at any time and from anywhere, eliminating the need for 
scheduling appointments or experiencing wait times, potentially facili￾tating more frequent symptom management and improved treatment 
outcomes (Tudor Car et al., 2020). Moreover, conversational agents can 
also reduce the financial burden associated with traditional symptom 
management approaches by minimizing the need for frequent in-person 
visits (Li et al., 2021). Additionally, people may feel more comfortable 
disclosing sensitive information to a dialog system, free from the fear of 
judgment or stigma, leading to more honest and accurate symptom 
reporting (He et al., 2023).

Few systematic reviews have explored the effectiveness of conver￾sational agents in managing symptoms. For example, systematic reviews 
conducted by He et al. (2023) and Lim et al. (2022) reported that 
conversational agent-based interventions were effective for managing 
mental health problems such as depression and anxiety. However, these 
systematic reviews have narrowly focused on psychological symptoms 
and have not adequately reported the characteristics of the conversa￾tional agents employed in the studies (He et al., 2023; Lim et al., 
2022). To address this research gap, the present study aims to conduct a sys￾tematic review of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to comprehen￾sively examine the characteristics and effectiveness of conversational 
agents delivered interventions in both physical and psychological 
symptom management. By providing a more comprehensive evaluation 
of conversational agents’ potential in managing a wide range of symp￾tom types across diverse populations, this review will provide compre￾hensive evidence to guide the development and optimization of more 
personalized, accessible, and efficient conversational agents for symp￾tom management.

## 2. Methods
## 2.1. Study protocol and registration
This systematic review was reported in accordance with the 
Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 
(PRISMA) checklist (Page et al., 2021a, 2021b). The review protocol 
was registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD42024502214).

## 2.2. Search strategy
A comprehensive literature search was conducted in Pubmed, ACM 
Digital Library, CINAHL, EMBASE, PyscInfo, Web of Science and Scopus 
from their inception to February 2024 and updated in Oct 2024. The 
search strategy combined three categories of terms: conversational 
agents, symptoms (including their hyponyms such as pain, nausea, and

depression), and randomized controlled trials. These terms were 
searched in the title and abstract fields using the structure: (conversa￾tional agent) AND (symptoms OR their hyponyms) AND (randomized 
controlled trial). The full search strategy is available in Supplementary 
Table 1. In addition, reference lists of relevant reviews, included studies 
and sources for gray literatures (Open Grey, OAlster grey repository, 
Bielefeld Academic Search Engine- BASE, and ProQuest Dissertations & 
Theses Global: The Sciences and Engineering Collection) were searched 
to identify additional eligible studies.

## 2.3. Inclusion and exclusion criteria
The inclusion and exclusion criteria were developed using the Pop￾ulation, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, and Study type (PICOS) 
framework (Schardt et al., 2007) as follows: Population: studies 
involving any population, including but not limited to patients with 
chronic diseases, cancer, or healthy individuals experiencing symptoms, 
were eligible. Intervention: any intervention involving a conversational 
agent in targeting symptom management. The conversational agent was 
defined as a dialog system using voice/speech and/or text (with or 
without visual output) that enables natural language conversations with 
users. It could be operated as a standalone software, a mobile applica￾tion, or a web browser-based application that is integrated in diverse 
platforms such as Facebook, WhatsApp, SMS, and virtual/embodied 
agents. Comparison: studies were eligible if they compared the 
conversational agent intervention to a control group, such as usual care 
and waitlist control. Outcomes: any symptom-related outcomes, such as 
symptom severity, frequency, and duration. Study type: Only RCTs were 
included in this systematic review. Studies were excluded if they (1) had 
only an abstract, (2) were study protocol or trial registrations, and (3) 
were non-English studies.

## 2.4. Screening and study selection
All studies researched from the databases were imported into 
EndNote (version X9, Clarivate Analytics), a reference management 
software. EndNote was also used to identify and remove any duplicate 
entries. The selection of studies was conducted in two stages. Initially, 
two independent reviewers (QLY and YZ) screened the titles and ab￾stracts of all identified studies in the EndNote to exclude studies that did 
not meet the basic inclusion criteria. Subsequently, the full texts of the 
remaining studies were retrieved for a detailed assessment against the 
inclusion and exclusion criteria by the same two reviewers. Any dis￾agreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion or, if 
necessary, third-party (YJX) consultation.

## 2.5. Data extraction
Data extraction was meticulously performed by a single reviewer 
(QLY) and subsequently verified by an independent second reviewer 
(YZ) to ensure accuracy. The extraction process was systematized and 
data was comprehensively recorded into an Excel spreadsheet. The in￾formation targeted fell into two main categories. First, basic study 
characteristics, which included authorship, country, study design, study 
objectives, population, sample size, symptom type, symptom assessment 
tool, control, feasibility measurement, user engagement and feedback, 
and main findings. Second, conversational agent details, which 
encompassed the names of the conversational agent, healthcare settings, 
platform, key element or theory, intervention duration, system devel￾opment, dialog system methods, AI techniques, dialog initiatives, input 
and output modality.

## 2.6. Data analysis
A narrative approach was employed to synthesize the data, focusing 
on the characteristics of included studies and involved conversational 
agents, study feasibility, user engagement and feedback, and effective￾ness for symptom management. Meta-analysis was not conducted due to 
the heterogeneity among the included studies regarding key interven￾tion elements of conversational agents, target symptoms, assessment 
tools, and study populations. A bubble chart was created to visualize the 
effectiveness of conversational agent-delivered interventions on symp￾tom management, with the X-axis representing different symptoms, the 
Y-axis representing individual studies, the bubble size indicating sample 
size, and bubble color denoting statistical significance.

## 2.7. Quality assessment
The Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool was used to assess the risk of bias 
(Sterne et al., 2019). This tool scrutinizes potential biases across five 
domains, including the randomization process, deviations from inten￾ded interventions, handling of missing outcome data, adequacy of 
outcome measurement, and selection of reported results. For each of the 
five domains, a meticulous rating was assigned, indicating either a low 
risk of bias, a high risk of bias, or some concerns. The overall risk of bias 
was then determined based on the ratings across all domains. In general, 
the overall risk of bias corresponded to the worst risk of bias identified in 
any of the domains (Sterne et al., 2019). Two independent reviewers 
(QLY and YZ) undertook this assessment, resolving any disagreements 
through discussion or the intervention of a third reviewer (YJX) when 
necessary.

## 3. Results
## 3.1. Study selection
The seven databases were searched and 2432 articles were retrieved 
for review. After removing duplicates, 1844 articles were screened by 
title and abstract, excluding 1771 articles. The remaining 73 articles 
underwent full-text review, resulting in the inclusion of 26 studies (Fig. 1). An additional 216 articles were identified from gray literature 
databases, and 108 articles from relevant citations. After removing du￾plicates and assessing titles, abstracts, and full texts, an extra three ar￾ticles were identified and included (Fig. 1). In total, 29 articles were 
included in this systematic review (Fig. 1).

## 3.4. Feasibility of included studies
Recruitment and completion rates were reported across the included 
studies (Table 1). The recruitment rate ranged from 10 to 100 % with a 
median of 72 %. Four studies successfully recruited 100 % of eligible 
participants (Anan et al., 2021; Jang et al., 2021; Klos et al., 2021; 
Tawfik et al., 2023). The overall completion rate ranged from 34 to 100 
% with a median of 79 %. Three studies had a 100 % overall completion 
rate (Maeda et al., 2020; Nicol et al., 2022; Tawfik et al., 2023). 
Completion rates were balanced between intervention and control 
groups. The intervention group completion rate ranged from 29 to 100 
% with a median of 82 % while the control group ranged from 40 to 100 
% with a median of 80 %.

## 3.5. User engagement and feedback of conversational agent
The studies demonstrated varied levels of engagement with the 
conversational agents (Table 1). Most reported short-term involvement 
ranging from a single interaction total to 25 sessions daily, with average 
interaction times between less than one minute to 11 min per session. 
One study reported participants on average used the app for 6 days per 
week, completing 55 mood checks and 313 messages sent (Nicol et al., 
2022). Another study showed an adherence rate of 71 % for 200 con￾versations initiated by the system (Hauser-Ulrich et al., 2020).

Most studies reported positive overall user experiences with the 
conversational agents. Attributes like ease of use were agreed by 84 %– 
94 % users, fun 63 %–76 %, comfortable 80 %, acceptable 51 %–80 %, 
feasible 65 %–70 %, satisfaction 70 %–91 %, and effectiveness/useful￾ness by 47 %–94 %. However, feedback varied in aspects of experience. 
Qualitative feedback praised aspects like reliable and informative con￾tent, emotion expression, interesting and personalized support by the 
chatbots (He et al., 2022; Oh et al., 2020; Ulrich et al., 2024b). Technical 
issues such as lagging, unnatural conversation flow and system crashes 
hindered smooth interactions and negatively impacted user experiences 
(He et al., 2022; Hunt et al., 2021; Jang et al., 2021; Maeda et al., 2020). 
Content issues were also reported, with some users finding the responses 
to be repetitive, generic, insufficiently profound, or lacking in depth 
(Burton et al., 2016; He et al., 2022; Hunt et al., 2021; Oh et al., 2020).

## 3.6. Effectiveness for symptom management
The effectiveness of conversational agent-delivered interventions on 
symptom management was visually presented in Fig. 2. Overall, these 
interventions demonstrated significant efficacy in symptom manage￾ment, with 39 statistical significant effects observed from 22 studies 
(Fig. 2). Regarding physical outcomes, conversational agents signifi￾cantly alleviated the physical chemotherapy-related side effects (Tawfik 
et al., 2023), gastrointestinal symptoms (Hunt et al., 2021), eating dis￾orders (Fitzsimmons-Craft et al., 2022) pain (Anan et al., 2021; Blasco 
et al., 2024) and somatic symptoms (Ulrich et al., 2024a, 2024b). In 
terms of psychological symptoms, twelve studies reported significant 
reductions in depression following conversational agent-based 
interventions (Burton et al., 2016; Chan et al., 2024; Fitzpatrick et al., 
2017; Fulmer et al., 2018; He et al., 2022; Hunt et al., 2021; Kleinau 
et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2022; MacNeill et al., 2024; Suharwardy et al., 
2023; Ulrich et al., 2024a, 2024b) (Fig. 2; Table 1). Similarly, ten studies 
observed significant effects on reducing anxiety symptoms(Chan et al., 
2024; Fitzpatrick et al., 2017; Fulmer et al., 2018; Greer et al., 2019; 
Hunt et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022; MacNeill et al., 2024; Maeda et al., 
2020; Ulrich et al., 2024a; Yahagi et al., 2024) (Fig. 2; Table 1). 
Furthermore, the interventions effectively attenuated stress (Ulrich et al., 2024a, 2024b), mental wellbeing (Ulrich et al., 2024a), the 
severity of fear of food symptoms (Hunt et al., 2021), panic symptoms 
(Oh et al., 2020), attention deficit symptoms (Jang et al., 2021) and 
psychological chemotherapy side effects (Tawfik et al., 2023) (Fig. 2; 
Table 1). One study also found that the conversational agent signifi￾cantly decreased the severity of physical and psychological symptoms of 
cancer-related symptoms (Schmitz et al., 2023) (Fig. 2; Table 1). Four￾teen analyses reported Cohen’s d values, indicating the effect sizes 
ranged from small (Cohen’s d = 0.35) to large (Cohen’s d = 1.47) (Chan 
et al., 2024; Hunt et al., 2021; Ulrich et al., 2024a). The details of the 
effect size are presented in Table 1.

## 4. Discussion
This is the first systematic review of RCTs to investigate conversa￾tional agents in the management of both physiological and psychologi￾cal symptoms. Our systematic review illustrates that conversational 
agents-based interventions were not only feasible and acceptable but 
also effective across various symptomatologies. These findings sub￾stantiate the potential for integrating conversational agents into broader 
healthcare frameworks.

The studies encompassed within this review span from 2020 to 2024, 
with a proportion of studies adopting a pilot RCT design. Moreover, 
nearly half of the studies assessed the feasibility or acceptability of 
conversational agent-based interventions alongside their effectiveness, 
underlining the nascent nature of the field. Geographically, a concen￾tration of research activity in developed countries, such as the United 
States, aligns with trends observed in other reviews of chatbot in￾terventions (Bin Sawad et al., 2022; Kocaballi et al., 2019; Laranjo et al., 
2018; Li et al., 2023; Milne-Ives et al., 2020; Schachner et al., 2020). 
Furthermore, the high risk of bias observed in approximately 60 % of the 
included studies indicated an urgent need for more robust study designs.

Conversational agents were implemented across multiple platforms 
including dedicated mobile applications and existing communication 
channels like WeChat and Facebook Messenger (He et al., 2022; Klos 
et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022). They utilized various external resources 
such as Microsoft Bot Framework and Azure Bot Service, and the Alexa 
ecosystem, which signifies the versatility of conversational agents 
(Ehrlich et al., 2023; Tawfik et al., 2023). The predominant rule-based 
dialog system methods could potentially limit the naturalness and 
responsiveness of the conversations (Menon and Shilpa, 2023). Notably, 
natural language processing (NLP) was the most frequently employed AI 
technology in the included studies, owing to its capacity to facilitate 
human-like interactions and understand user intent (Eysenbach, 2023; 
Ray, 2023). Most of the conversational agents reported in the included 
studies primarily support text-based interaction, with only a minority 
leveraging voice or visual inputs and outputs. However, for populations 
in less developed regions, particularly among individuals with lower 
levels of literacy, conversational agents capable of processing voice or 
visual information could be far more accessible and in higher demand 
(Dingler et al., 2021). The general lack of depth in reporting on the 
development techniques of conversational agents was observed across 
the studies. While most studies claimed to utilize AI-based systems, the 
paucity of technical details impedes the establishment of a generic sys￾tem architecture, which is crucial for the development and structure of 
AI-based conversational agents in symptom management (Schachner 
et al., 2020).

The included studies reported high completion rates, with a median 
of 79 % and three studies achieving a 100 % completion rate. These 
findings indicate a strong user engagement with conversational agents 
in symptom management. Notably, the completion rate observed in this 
review was higher than the pooled completion rate of 57 % reported in a 
previous meta-analysis of app-based interventions for chronic condi￾tions (Meyerowitz-Katz et al., 2020). This higher completion rate may be 
attributed to the convenience and accessibility offered by conversational 
agents, which may reduce barriers to participation commonly encoun￾tered in traditional healthcare settings (Milne-Ives et al., 2020). 
Furthermore, the majority of studies indicated that users had positive 
experiences with conversational agents, providing a compelling argu￾ment for their viability and acceptability in symptom management.

A substantial portion of included studies reported that conversa￾tional agents had a significant impact on symptom management, 
particularly in alleviating symptoms of depression and anxiety 
(Burton et al., 2016; Danieli et al., 2022; Ehrlich et al., 2023; Fitzpatrick et al., 2017; Fulmer et al., 2018; He et al., 2022; Klos et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022; Nicol et al., 2022; Suharwardy et al., 2023). However, the primary 
outcomes reported were relatively narrow in scope, predominantly 
focusing on depression and/or anxiety symptoms. CBT, a well￾established therapeutic approach that is amenable to digital adapta￾tion, was commonly embedded within these conversational agents (He 
et al., 2023; Lim et al., 2022). Given the structured and goal-oriented 
nature of CBT, it lends itself to integration within mobile applications, 
as evidenced by prior research demonstrating its effectiveness in digital 
formats (Stawarz et al., 2018). Moreover, there is a paucity of conver￾sational agents targeting physical symptoms, such as pain management, 
possibly due to the complex nature of physical symptom assessment and 
intervention through digital means (Martinengo et al., 2022). Despite 
the promising findings, it is important to note that four studies included 
in this review found that some participants experienced severe symp￾toms during the conversational agent intervention, and subsequently 
transitioned to standard treatment such as face-to-face professional 
psychological counseling, suggesting that conversational agents may not 
be able to entirely replace traditional treatments in all cases (Danieli 
et al., 2022; He et al., 2022; Schmitz et al., 2023; Sikorskii et al., 2018).

## 4.1. Implications for practice and research
The effectiveness of conversational agents across various symptom￾atologies, along with their high user engagement and positive experi￾ences, underscores their potential for integration into broader 
healthcare frameworks. However, conversational agents should be 
viewed as complementary tools rather than replacements for traditional 
treatments, necessitating the establishment of well-defined protocols for 
identifying and referring cases that require human intervention. 
Healthcare providers should carefully consider the specific needs and 
preferences of their patient populations when implementing conversa￾tional agents, including factors such as literacy levels, access to tech￾nology, and cultural considerations. The development and deployment 
of conversational agents should involve close collaboration among 
healthcare professionals, AI experts, and end-users to ensure that the 
systems are clinically relevant, user-friendly, and aligned with best 
practices in symptom management. Moreover, policymakers and orga￾nizations should prioritize guidelines and standards addressing data 
privacy, security, and ethical considerations.

From a research perspective, several key areas warrant further 
investigation. More robust study designs, rigorous methodologies and 
larger sample sizes are needed to provide high-quality evidence on the 
effectiveness of conversational agents. These studies should extend their 
geographical scope beyond developed nations, addressing the disparity 
in healthcare resources and accessibility in low- and middle-income 
countries. Additionally, the development of more advanced dialog 
system methods, such as deep learning, and the incorporation of mul￾tiple modalities (text, voice, visual, etc.) should be prioritized to 
enhance the naturalness, adaptability, and accessibility of conversa￾tional agents. This will enable the creation of more intelligent and in￾clusive systems that cater to a broader range of users, particularly those 
with lower levels of literacy or limited access to technology. Further￾more, future research should focus on expanding the range of symptoms 
addressed by conversational agents, particularly in the domain of 
physical symptom management. Finally, to facilitate replication and 
transparency, researchers must provide comprehensive technical de￾scriptions of the AI-based conversational systems employed, enabling 
the establishment of a generic system architecture and accelerating the 
development of more effective conversational agents.

## 4.2. Strengths and limitations
Our systematic review had several strengths. First, this review 
encompassed a wide range of physical and psychological symptoms, 
providing an overview of the conversational agents in symptom man￾agement. Second, it offered an in-depth analysis of the characteristics 
and effectiveness of conversational agents, with detailed data extraction 
on key intervention elements, AI techniques, and dialog system 
methods, enabling a comprehensive understanding of the current state 
of research.

Despite these strengths, several limitations must be acknowledged. 
The heterogeneity of the included studies in terms of symptom type, 
intervention components, outcome measures, and interactive duration 
presents challenges in conducting meta-analysis and drawing general￾izable conclusions. The focus on English-language publications may also 
introduce publication bias, potentially overlooking significant studies 
conducted in non-English speaking regions. Additionally, the rapid 
evolution of AI technology, such as advancements in natural language 
processing and dialog management, suggests that some of the conver￾sational agents included in this review may already be technologically 
outdated (Casu et al., 2024). This highlights the need for ongoing 
research to ensure the currency of findings in this dynamic domain. 
Lastly, the review did not consistently capture the long-term efficacy 
and adherence to conversational agent interventions, which is crucial 
for understanding their role in sustained symptom management.

## 5. Conclusions
This systematic review suggests that conversational agents can play a 
beneficial role in the management of physical and psychological 
symptoms. The positive user engagement and experiences reported 
across multiple studies indicate the potential of conversational agents to 
complement traditional healthcare delivery. However, caution is war￾ranted due to high risk of bias in the reviewed studies. Future research 
should prioritize improving the methodological quality of RCTs to 
minimize potential biases and further strengthen the evidence base.

## 3.7. Risk-of-bias and quality assessments
The risk of bias in each included study measured by the Risk of Bias 
2.0 tool was presented in Fig. 3. Only three studies had an overall low 
risk of bias (Fulmer et al., 2018; Tawfik et al., 2023; Yahagi et al., 2024), 
while seventeen exhibited a high risk of bias (Danieli et al., 2022; Ehr￾lich et al., 2023; Fitzpatrick et al., 2017; Fitzsimmons-Craft et al., 2022; 
Greer et al., 2019; He et al., 2022; Jang et al., 2021; Klos et al., 2021; Liu 
et al., 2022; MacNeill et al., 2024; Oh et al., 2020; Schmitz et al., 2023; 
Sikorskii et al., 2018; Suharwardy et al., 2023; Ulrich et al., 2024a, 
2024b; Yahagi et al., 2024) and another nine raised some concerns (Anan et al., 2021; Blasco et al., 2024; Burton et al., 2016; Chan et al., 2024; Hauser-Ulrich et al., 2020; Hunt et al., 2021; Kleinau et al., 2024; Maeda et al., 2020; Nicol et al., 2022). Twenty-four studies presented a 
low risk of bias in the randomization process (Anan et al., 2021; Burton 
et al., 2016; Danieli et al., 2022; Fitzpatrick et al., 2017; Fitzsimmons￾Craft et al., 2022; Fulmer et al., 2018; Greer et al., 2019; Hauser-Ulrich 
et al., 2020; He et al., 2022; Hunt et al., 2021; Jang et al., 2021; Kleinau 
et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2022; MacNeill et al., 2024; Maeda et al., 2020; 
Nicol et al., 2022; Oh et al., 2020; Sikorskii et al., 2018; Suharwardy 
et al., 2023; Tawfik et al., 2023; Ulrich et al., 2024a, 2024b; Yasukawa 
et al., 2024). The predominant rule-based dialog system methods could potentially limit the naturalness and responsiveness of the conversations (Menon and Shilpa, 2023). Notably, natural language processing (NLP) was the most frequently employed AI technology in the included studies, owing to its capacity to facilitate human-like interactions and understand user intent (Eysenbach, 2023; Ray, 2023). Most of the conversational agents reported in the included studies primarily support text-based interaction, with only a minority leveraging voice or visual inputs and outputs. However, for populations in less developed regions, particularly among individuals with lower levels of literacy, conversational agents capable of processing voice or visual information could be far more accessible and in higher demand (Dingler et al., 2021). The general lack of depth in reporting on the development techniques of conversational agents was observed across the studies. While most studies claimed to utilize AI-based systems, the paucity of technical details impedes the establishment of a generic sys￾tem architecture, which is crucial for the development and structure of 
AI-based conversational agents in symptom management (Schachner 
et al., 2020).

The included studies reported high completion rates, with a median 
of 79 % and three studies achieving a 100 % completion rate. These 
findings indicate a strong user engagement with conversational agents 
in symptom management. Notably, the completion rate observed in this 
review was higher than the pooled completion rate of 57 % reported in a 
previous meta-analysis of app-based interventions for chronic condi￾tions (Meyerowitz-Katz et al., 2020). This higher completion rate may be 
attributed to the convenience and accessibility offered by conversational 
agents, which may reduce barriers to participation commonly encoun￾tered in traditional healthcare settings (Milne-Ives et al., 2020). 
Furthermore, the majority of studies indicated that users had positive 
experiences with conversational agents, providing a compelling argu￾ment for their viability and acceptability in symptom management.

A substantial portion of included studies reported that conversa￾tional agents had a significant impact on symptom management, 
particularly in alleviating symptoms of depression and anxiety 
(Burton et al., 2016; Danieli et al., 2022; Ehrlich et al., 2023; Fitzpatrick et al., 2017; Fulmer et al., 2018; He et al., 2022; Klos et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022; Nicol et al., 2022; Suharwardy et al., 2023). However, the primary 
outcomes reported were relatively narrow in scope, predominantly 
focusing on depression and/or anxiety symptoms. CBT, a well￾established therapeutic approach that is amenable to digital adapta￾tion, was commonly embedded within these conversational agents (He 
et al., 2023; Lim et al., 2022). Given the structured and goal-oriented 
nature of CBT, it lends itself to integration within mobile applications, 
as evidenced by prior research demonstrating its effectiveness in digital 
formats (Stawarz et al., 2018). Moreover, there is a paucity of conver￾sational agents targeting physical symptoms, such as pain management, 
possibly due to the complex nature of physical symptom assessment and 
intervention through digital means (Martinengo et al., 2022). Despite 
the promising findings, it is important to note that four studies included 
in this review found that some participants experienced severe symp￾toms during the conversational agent intervention, and subsequently 
transitioned to standard treatment such as face-to-face professional 
psychological counseling, suggesting that conversational agents may not 
be able to entirely replace traditional treatments in all cases (Danieli 
et al., 2022; He et al., 2022; Schmitz et al., 2023; Sikorskii et al., 2018).

## 4.1. Implications for practice and research
The effectiveness of conversational agents across various symptom￾atologies, along with their high user engagement and positive experi￾ences, underscores their potential for integration into broader 
healthcare frameworks. However, conversational agents should be 
viewed as complementary tools rather than replacements for traditional 
treatments, necessitating the establishment of well-defined protocols for 
identifying and referring cases that require human intervention. 
Healthcare providers should carefully consider the specific needs and 
preferences of their patient populations when implementing conversa￾tional agents, including factors such as literacy levels, access to tech￾nology, and cultural considerations. The development and deployment 
of conversational agents should involve close collaboration among 
healthcare professionals, AI experts, and end-users to ensure that the 
systems are clinically relevant, user-friendly, and aligned with best 
practices in symptom management. Moreover, policymakers and orga￾nizations should prioritize guidelines and standards addressing data 
privacy, security, and ethical considerations.

From a research perspective, several key areas warrant further 
investigation. More robust study designs, rigorous methodologies and 
larger sample sizes are needed to provide high-quality evidence on the 
effectiveness of conversational agents. These studies should extend their 
geographical scope beyond developed nations, addressing the disparity 
in healthcare resources and accessibility in low- and middle-income 
countries. Additionally, the development of more advanced dialog 
system methods, such as deep learning, and the incorporation of mul￾tiple modalities (text, voice, visual, etc.) should be prioritized to 
enhance the naturalness, adaptability, and accessibility of conversa￾tional agents. This will enable the creation of more intelligent and in￾clusive systems that cater to a broader range of users, particularly those 
with lower levels of literacy or limited access to technology. Further￾more, future research should focus on expanding the range of symptoms 
addressed by conversational agents, particularly in the domain of 
physical symptom management. Finally, to facilitate replication and 
transparency, researchers must provide comprehensive technical de￾scriptions of the AI-based conversational systems employed, enabling 
the establishment of a generic system architecture and accelerating the 
development of more effective conversational agents.

## 4.2. Strengths and limitations
Our systematic review had several strengths. First, this review 
encompassed a wide range of physical and psychological symptoms, 
providing an overview of the conversational agents in symptom man￾agement. Second, it offered an in-depth analysis of the characteristics 
and effectiveness of conversational agents, with detailed data extraction 
on key intervention elements, AI techniques, and dialog system 
methods, enabling a comprehensive understanding of the current state 
of research.

Despite these strengths, several limitations must be acknowledged. 
The heterogeneity of the included studies in terms of symptom type, 
intervention components, outcome measures, and interactive duration 
presents challenges in conducting meta-analysis and drawing general￾izable conclusions. The focus on English-language publications may also 
introduce publication bias, potentially overlooking significant studies 
conducted in non-English speaking regions. Additionally, the rapid 
evolution of AI technology, such as advancements in natural language 
processing and dialog management, suggests that some of the conver￾sational agents included in this review may already be technologically 
outdated (Casu et al., 2024). This highlights the need for ongoing 
research to ensure the currency of findings in this dynamic domain. 
Lastly, the review did not consistently capture the long-term efficacy 
and adherence to conversational agent interventions, which is crucial 
for understanding their role in sustained symptom management.

## 5. Conclusions
This systematic review suggests that conversational agents can play a 
beneficial role in the management of physical and psychological 
symptoms. The positive user engagement and experiences reported 
across multiple studies indicate the potential of conversational agents to 
complement traditional healthcare delivery. However, caution is war￾ranted due to high risk of bias in the reviewed studies. Future research 
should prioritize improving the methodological quality of RCTs to 
minimize potential biases and further strengthen the evidence base.
