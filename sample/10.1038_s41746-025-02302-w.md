## Effectiveness of Large Language Models in Preoperative and Discharge Education: A Systematic Review Based on an Evaluation Framework

Mengting Wang1, Haoming Ma1* , Meihua Piao1*

1 School of Nursing, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences & Peking Union Medical College, Beijing, China

* Corresponding authors:  
Haoming Ma, Email: haoming_ma@student.pumc.edu.cn  
Meihua Piao, Email: parkmihua@gmail.com

### Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly incorporated into preoperative and discharge education, yet their effectiveness and the ways in which they are evaluated remain inconsistent. This systematic review assessed the effectiveness of LLM-based interventions and identified evidence gaps relevant to understanding how model characteristics may influence patient outcomes. We searched five databases from inception to April 18, 2025, ultimately including twenty studies. Outcomes were narratively synthesized, and interventions were evaluated using a published four-dimension framework, with reporting patterns visualized through a heatmap. Many studies reported benefits for anxiety reduction and selected satisfaction domains, whereas findings for pain, recovery, and other satisfaction elements showed no significant differences from conventional materials. Reporting of evaluation sub-dimensions was uneven, with trustworthiness and performance rarely documented alongside clinical endpoints. These gaps highlight the need for future research that integrates model-centric and patient-centric evaluations to support responsible clinical deployment.

### Introduction

Effective preoperative education and discharge instructions are critical components of patient-centered care1.  
These educational interventions have been shown to improve psychological outcomes, including significant reductions in preoperative and postoperative anxiety2 and increased satisfaction with care3. In parallel, they also enhance physiological outcomes, such asimproved self-management capacity 4 and better adherence to treatment regimens5. Despite their benefits, the effectiveness of these strategies is often compromised by barriers like language proficiency, cultural mismatches, and low health literacy, particularly in fast-paced clinical environments where one-size-fits-all communication strategies fall short of meeting diverse patient needs6.

Moreover, a shortage of trained nursing staff further undermines the implementation of individualized patient education, as limited human resources constrain both the time and continuity required for effective educational engagement7.

In response to these limitations, large language models (LLMs) have emerged as a potential adjunct in patient education8. For instance, Dihan et al.9 found that ChatGPT-generated materials for pediatric cataract education were rated as high in quality, accuracy, readability, and understandability, while also adapting well to caregiver-specific concerns. These findings suggest the potential of LLMs to supplement traditional clinical communication methods.

Existing studies on the use of LLMs in preoperative education and discharge instructions have yielded inconsistent findings. For instance, Gan et al.10 reported significant improvements in patient satisfaction and reductions in anxiety following ChatGPT-based preoperative education, whereas Huang et al.11 found no significant difference between LLM-generated and standard discharge instructions in key satisfaction domains such as pending test results and incidental findings. In addition to these divergent outcomes, the current evidence base remains scattered and methodologically heterogeneous, with wide variations in intervention design, outcome measures, and evaluation criteria. To date, no systematic review has comprehensively synthesized the effectiveness of LLM-based interventions in these two key clinical scenarios.

Moreover, while a few studies have begun to incorporate structured evaluation frameworks to assess LLM-generated educational content, these frameworks have primarily been applied to task-level outputs rather than to the internal characteristics of the models12. As a result, it remains unclear how specific LLM characteristics may affect patient-centered outcomes in clinical communication.

Since the use of LLMs in clinical contexts is still in its early stages, the existing body of evidence is not yet sufficient to answer this question. To guide future research, it is important to first understand how different LLM characteristics and patient outcomes have been reported in the current literature, and where the reporting gaps lie.

To that end, this systematic review evaluates the effectiveness of LLM-based interventions and maps the reporting of model characteristics and patient outcomes in preoperative and discharge education, using the structured evaluation framework proposed by Abbasian et al.13 By synthesizing both quantitative and qualitative findings, we aim to assess the potential of LLMs in clinical education and clarify key reporting gaps to guide future research. Rather than inferring causal relationships, our goal is to inform the evidence base for the use of LLMs in patient education. This review offers practical insights for developers, clinicians, and policymakers considering their implementation in healthcare settings.

## Results

### Study selection

A total of 195 records were initially identified through database searches, including PubMed (n=55), Embase (n=86), CINAHL Plus (n=17), arXiv (n=31), and IEEE Xplore (n=6). After removing 36 duplicates, 159 records were screened by title and abstract. Forty-six full-text studies were assessed for eligibility, and 24 were excluded due to reasons such as not focusing on large language models or generative AI, not addressing patient education, being non-empirical, or lacking relevance to discharge or preoperative contexts. In addition, 5 more studies were identified through citation searching and included after full-text assessment. Ultimately, 20 studies were included in the final synthesis. ### Study characteristics

The included studies (n=20) comprised randomized controlled trials (n=3), cross-sectional studies (n=16), and one quasi-experimental study. Regarding funding, two studies reported industry or personal financial support11,14, seven studies reported institutional or government research funding10,15-20, and the remaining did not report any funding source21-31. All studies employed ChatGPT as one of the LLMs. In terms of task type, ten studies focused on preoperative education10,17,18,21,22,24-26,29,30, nine on discharge instruction11,14-16,19,23,27,28,31, and one study addressed both20. The end-user types involved included real11 or simulated patients16, simulated families15, doctors31, and experts17. The task domains represented in the included studies were also diverse, spanning emergency medicine11, orthopedic surgery10 and head and neck cancer24, among others. Comparison benchmarks varied across studies. Twelve studies used expert rating16,17,19-24,27-30, while others compared ChatGPT with standard human-delivered instructions or institutional materials10,11,15,18,25,26. Two studies did not clearly report the benchmark 14,31. A detailed summary of study characteristics is provided in Table 1.

### Study quality

Quality appraisal indicated variation across the 20 included studies (Table 2). The three RCTs met over 10 of 13 JBI criteria and satisfied critical items required for interpreting intervention–outcome differences; the few unmet items largely confined to insufficient details on randomization or blinding. Cross-sectional studies typically fulfilled at least 6 of 8 quality indicators, with most omissions related to confounding. The single quasi-experimental study fulfilled several criteria but did not meet all critical items.

To be more specific, four studies—Mika et al. (2023)21, Mika et al. (2024)22, Lee et al. (2024)24 and Baskin et al. (2025)31—did not meet the prespecified critical items for their respective designs. Their reporting provided limited detail on key aspects of the intervention or outcome assessment, making it impossible to determine whether the observed findings reflected the LLM-based educational component. These studies were therefore excluded in the effectiveness synthesis but remained eligible for the framework-based mapping of LLM characteristics and patient outcomes. The quality assessment results are summarized in Table 2, with detailed evaluation processes provided in Supplementary Table 1.

### Outcome domains

This review organized reported outcomes into two overarching categories: physiological/behavioral outcomes and psychological outcomes. The physiological/behavioral category included functional recovery and pain perception. The psychological category encompassed satisfaction scores, perceived comprehension, anxiety scores, depression scores, communication preference, and educational coverage.

### Satisfaction scores

Across the three included studies, all of which were rated as high quality by the JBI appraisal，satisfaction scores generally favored LLM-generated materials. In Huang et al. (2024)11, which evaluated seven domains of emergency-department discharge communication, ChatGPT-generated instructions received higher favorable ratings in several areas, including explanations of procedures (LLM: 75.9% vs. Standard: 54.8%) and treatment plans (LLM: 85.5% vs. Standard: 68.5%). Gan et al. (2025)10 also reported higher satisfaction in the LLM group for both preoperative education (LLM: 4.22 ± 0.51 vs. Standard: 3.43 ± 0.84) and the overall hospitalization experience (LLM: 4.11 ± 0.65 vs. Standard: 3.46 ± 0.69).

However, not all domains showed the same pattern. In Huang et al. (2024)11, Standard discharge instructions were rated slightly more favorably for testing received in the emergency department (LLM: 53.0% vs. Standard: 56.2%), pending test results (LLM: 30.1% vs. Standard: 34.2%) and incidental findings (LLM: 31.3% vs. Standard: 34.2%). Differences between the ChatGPT-generated and surgeon-written leaflets in Lockie and Choi (2024)26 were minimal (LLM: 7.5 vs. Standard: 7.1 on an 8-point scale), yielding no identifiable direction of effect.

From the perspective of how the interventions were delivered, differences in study design and the role assigned to LLMs contributed substantially to the variation in satisfaction outcomes. The study designs ranged from a double-blind randomized controlled trial (RCT)11 to a single-blind pilot RCT10, with the third study using a cross-sectional assessment26. Differences in LLM involvement provided another source of heterogeneity. In Huang et al.11, GPT-4 generated complete discharge-instruction documents from structured emergency department notes, while Gan et al.10 used ChatGPT-4 as a supplementary tool within live informed-consent discussions. Further, the LLM contribution was limited to a single leaflet drafted from one prompt in Lockie & Choi26. Satisfaction differences were smallest where LLM involvement was lowest.

From the perspective of who was evaluated and how satisfaction was defined and measured, population characteristics, satisfaction construct, measurement instruments, and comparator benchmarks introduced additional sources of variation. Population characteristics differed in ways that could influence how participants interacted with the materials. Huang et al.11 relied on Amazon MTurk respondents who served as simulated patient readers, whereas the other two studies involved real clinical populations, including older adults preparing for total knee arthroplasty in Gan et al.10 and patients recovering from laparoscopic cholecystectomy in Lockie and Choi26. Both Huang et al.11 and Gan et al.10 worked with populations that tended to have low health literacy, although this information was not reported in Lockie and Choi26. The studies also varied in the satisfaction constructs they assessed and the tools they used. Huang et al.11 focused on domain-specific components of discharge communication using a multi-domain Likert matrix, Gan et al.10 emphasized overall experience using five-point items, and Lockie and Choi26 evaluated a single leaflet with an eight-item validated instrument. Comparator conditions also varied. Huang et al.11 used an institutional standardized template, whereas both Gan et al.10 and Lockie and Choi26 relied on surgeon-led materials, reflecting different communication baselines.

### Anxiety scores

Across the two studies that evaluated anxiety, both reported reductions following LLM-supported education. In the trial by Gan et al.10, patients who received ChatGPT-based explanations showed consistently lower anxiety than those receiving standard informed-consent discussions. This pattern appeared across multiple validated instruments, including the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scales for Anxiety (HADS-A), and the Visual Analogue Scale for Anxiety (VAS-A), with significant reductions observed both before and after surgery. Yahagi et al.18 found a similar trend using the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI). Anxiety levels in the intervention group declined after the ChatGPT-supported consultation and remained lower after surgery, whereas scores in the control group showed little change.

### Additional outcomes

The remaining outcomes were each reported in a single study. Function recovery and pain perception did not differ meaningfully between groups10. Depression scores, measured with the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scales for Depression (HADS-D), were modestly lower after the ChatGPT-based intervention10. Perceived comprehension showed a mixed pattern across discharge domains. GPT-generated materials received higher ratings for procedures, treatment, medications, self-care, follow-up, and return precautions, whereas ratings for testing, pending results, and incidental findings were similar or slightly higher for standard instructions11. Communication preference varied by language, with ChatGPT-rated translations comparable to professional versions for Spanish and Portuguese but less adequate for Haitian Creole15. Educational coverage improved with GPT-4–generated patient-centered letters, though some safety-related recommendations were omitted19.

### Framework-based mapping of moderating dimensions

Table 3 summarizes how evaluation sub-dimensions and outcome types were reported across the 20 included studies, with specific details provided in Supplementary Table 5. Within the evaluation framework13, accuracy and trustworthiness were the most documented. In contrast, empathy as well as performance appeared far less frequently.

For outcome types, most studies focused on psychological measures like satisfaction and anxiety scores. A smaller group described physiological or behavioral outcomes, including pain perception and functional recovery. Overall, satisfaction and anxiety scores tended to have a larger number of reported evaluation sub-dimensions, particularly from the empathy and accuracy domains. On the other hand, outcomes such as communication preference and perceived comprehension were accompanied by a narrower set of reported evaluation criteria.

To provide an overview of how reporting was distributed across outcome types, Figure 2 visualized the presence of each evaluation sub-dimension. This figure is intended to depict reporting patterns only, without implying comparisons across studies or relationships between evaluation focus and outcomes.

### Accuracy

Accuracy sub-dimensions exhibited the highest levels of co-reported with outcome types. Intrinsic metrics and SSI were most frequently appeared in studies assessing satisfaction scores and anxiety scores. In contrast, other sub-dimensions such as conciseness, up-to-dateness, robustness, and groundedness were rarely co-reported with specific outcomes.

### Trustworthiness

Within the trustworthiness domain, interpretability and bias were the most commonly reported with outcomes. These two sub-dimensions occasionally appeared together. However, safety and security were only sporadically reported with communication preference and educational coverage and privacy showed little overlap with any outcome type.

### Empathy

Emotional support and personalization frequently appeared together in studies evaluating satisfaction scores and anxiety scores. Health literacy appeared less often, with isolated reports in studies of anxiety scores. Fairness was not reported in any included study.

### Performance

Sub-dimensions under the performance domain were rarely co-reported with any outcome type. Only memory efficiency is co-reported with educational coverage.

## Discussion

This systematic review synthesized current evidence on the use of LLMs in preoperative education and discharge instructions. Our primary aim was to summarize the effectiveness reported in existing studies and to provide a descriptive mapping of how evaluation dimensions have been reported, with the goal of making current evidence gaps visible. Overall, the findings indicate that LLMs, especially ChatGPT and its variants, have shown generally favorable results in several reported outcomes.

Among the twenty included studies, three reported patient satisfaction outcomes, with patterns that often favored LLM-supported materials but were not uniform across all domains10,11,26. These findings were observed despite differences in study design, LLM involvement, population characteristics, satisfaction construct, measurement instruments, and comparator benchmarks. Two studies evaluated anxiety, and both documented reductions following LLM-supported education10,18. Other outcomes were each reported in single studies, including functional recovery, pain perception, depression scores, perceived comprehension, communication preference, and educational coverage10,11,15,19. These isolated findings provide useful descriptive insights but do not allow for broader generalization.

Beyond outcome-level comparisons, our review revealed considerable variability in how evaluation dimensions were reported across studies (Table 3). Most interventions included reported elements of accuracy, empathy, and trustworthiness, especially sub-dimensions such as intrinsic metrics, SSI, emotional support, and personalization. However, performance dimensions were substantially underrepresented. Sub-dimensions such as memory efficiency, floating point operations, token limit, and number of parameters were rarely or not reported, even though these characteristics are frequently discussed in the broader LLM literature on real-world clinical deployment. This uneven coverage reflects the current emphasis of published studies on communicative quality rather than architectural or computational rigor.

Recent advances in healthcare-specific LLM development underscore why this gap is consequential. Taylor et al.32 demonstrated that when embedding spaces are optimized through domain-specific pretraining, even relatively small LLMs can outperform general-purpose models on clinical classification tasks. The HEAL framework further shows that refining embedding alignment without altering the generation module can significantly improve factual consistency and reduce hallucinations in retrieval-augmented clinical applications33. Meanwhile, Restrepo et al.34 focused on embedding learning strategies tailored for low-resource healthcare environments, where computational constraints and linguistic heterogeneity pose unique challenges. Their results show that embedding-level alignment techniques improve model robustness and factual grounding even under severe infrastructure limitations. Such findings suggest that technical underpinnings like embedding alignment and computational efficiency are integral to how LLMs perform in high-stakes care settings. We acknowledge that the variation in reported dimensions observed in our review likely reflects the differing objectives of the included studies rather than limitations in study quality. However, the limited reporting of performance characteristics leaves an important aspect of model behavior insufficiently described. As LLM applications in healthcare continue to expand, future evaluations may benefit from incorporating more systematic reporting of performance-level characteristics, particularly in settings where computational and infrastructural constraints may shape how models operate in practice.

After outlining how outcomes and evaluation dimensions were each reported, examining their points of overlap revealed several exploratory co-reporting patterns across the included studies. The co-reported patterns mostly reflect how outcome selection has followed researchers’ evaluation priorities. When viewed through the four-dimensional framework13, it becomes clear thatstudies examining empathy features, including emotional support and personalization, were more likely to report improvements in satisfaction10,11,26, anxiety outcomes10,18, and depression scores10. Yet current studies rarely examine whether other LLM features, especially those not obviously related to these outcomes, might also matter for those outcomes.

Within the accuracy domain, sub-dimensions such as intrinsic metrics and SSI were most commonly reported, especially in studies presenting satisfaction and anxiety scores10,11,26. These aspects focus on surface-level correctness and user-aligned plausibility. However, dimensions like robustness, generalization, conciseness, up-to-dateness and groundedness were reported far less frequently, leaving limited evidence on how LLMs behave in more dynamic or uncertain clinical contexts.

Busch et al.35 provide supporting evidence for this imbalance. In their synthesis of 86 studies, hallucinations were identified in 44.2% (38/86), confabulations in 20.9% (18/86), and specificity issues in 15.1% (13/86)—findings that align with our observation that evaluations often prioritize semantic match and surface-level plausibility. In contrast, outdated content was reported in 14.0% (12/86), and insufficient evidence, an indicator of weak factual grounding, in just 8.1% (7/86). This discrepancy reinforces the concern that assessments more often target what is easily measurable rather than what is clinically essential. While intrinsic metrics help capture user-facing experience, the lack of reporting on robustness and grounding leaves uncertainty regarding LLM reliability in high-stakes settings such as preoperative education and discharge planning.

It is worth noting that while our review identified no study that formally reported robustness as a predefined evaluation dimension, Busch et al.35 reported that 31.4% (27/86) of included studies exhibited issues related to prompt- or input-dependence, which they interpreted as a proxy for robustness concerns. This apparent contradiction reflects not a disagreement in findings but rather a divergence in analytical orientation and coding standards.

Specifically, Busch et al.35 adopted an output failure–oriented taxonomy that captures model vulnerabilities described in the literature, including prompt sensitivity, reduced specificity, and limited tolerance to complexity. In contrast, our analysis followed Abbasian et al.’s dimension-based evaluation framework13, in which robustness is narrowly defined as the model’s ability to maintain consistent performance under structured perturbations to prompts or inputs. As such, unless a study explicitly included robustness testing, such as comparative performance across varying prompt formulations, it was not coded as addressing this dimension.

This methodological divergence underscores a broader insight: although robustness concerns are frequently acknowledged in qualitative terms, they are rarely subjected to formal or quantitative evaluation in LLM studies targeting clinical communication. Future work should treat robustness testing as a deliberate component of study design to close this evaluative gap.

The trustworthiness dimension received moderate attention to bias and interpretability, but sub-dimensions related to privacy, safety and security were infrequently reported. This underrepresentation echoes findings from Bedi et al.36, who found that only a small proportion of studies addressed trust-related concerns like bias. Notably, their review did not report any evaluation related to privacy, suggesting that data protection remains an underexplored area in LLM evaluation. These patterns indicate that ethically critical aspects continue to receive limited empirical attention.

Empathy sub-dimensions, especially emotional support and personalization, frequently co-reported with favorable psychological outcomes. Yet, dimensions like fairness and health literacy were seldom reported. This observation is consistent with Sorin et al.37, who noted that most empathy evaluations emphasize emotional tone and supportive language, while rarely including equity-oriented constructs such as sociocultural alignment. Additionally, Zhou et al.38 likewise reported disparities in empathetic responses across demographic groups in low-resource settings, linked to language, age, and gender. Therefore, the underrepresentation of fairness and literacy in our review likely reflects not an accidental omission, but a broader methodological blind spot in current LLM evaluation efforts. Such findings highlight that without explicit consideration of fairness and health literacy, LLMs may reinforce structural inequities, thereby limiting the generalizability and ethical robustness of their applications across diverse patient populations.

Performance-related features such as memory efficiency, token limitations, and model size were almost entirely absent from evaluations. Bedi et al. 36 similarly noted this issue, finding that only 4.6% of the 519 studies in their analysis addressed deployment feasibility, a category that includes performance concerns such as cost, latency and inference runtime. This widespread omission likely reflects a predominant research focus on accuracy and content validity, while aspects such as deployment feasibility and computational efficiency remain insufficiently documented in the current literature.

The implications for clinical practice are multifaceted. A central implication emerging from this evidence is how LLMs should be positioned within patient education. In the context of preoperative and discharge education, LLMs are better suited as supportive tools for information delivery and emotional reassurance, rather than substitutes for clinician-led communication. Although multiple studies reported improvements in psychological outcomes such as patient satisfaction10,11,26 and anxiety reduction10,18, only one study mentioned physiological or behavioral outcomes10. This highlights the need for further evidence across a broader set of clinical endpoints before LLMs can be positioned within patient care workflows.

The review also identified critical blind spots in reported trustworthiness sub-dimensions like privacy, safety and security. The limited reporting of these aspects makes it difficult to determine how current LLM systems handle them, which in turn constrains the ethical appraisal and future scalability of LLM use in clinical environments.

As emphasized by Emani et al.39, human oversight mechanisms, including real-time auditing, clinician co-signatures, and patient feedback channels, are essential to ensure safe clinical deployment. Going forward, clinical institutions should establish clear governance protocols that define the roles of AI-generated content, assign human accountability for final communications, and implement structured monitoring systems to flag potential errors.

A further implication relates to how LLMs serve vulnerable patient groups, as the review also underscores the need for tailored LLM designs that accommodate their specific communication needs. Sub-dimensions related to health literacy and fairness were rarely documented, providing limited insight into how LLMs perform across diverse populations. Prior research has shown that patients with limited literacy or communication barriers often face disproportionate challenges in understanding health information40. In clinical practice, this means that when LLMs are considered for patient education, attention should be given to whether the systems offer features that support equitable and accessible communication, such as adjustable linguistic complexity, emotion-sensitive tone adjustments and culturally aligned phrasing.

Taken together, these implications suggest that introducing LLMs into clinical workflows should not aim to replace human communication, but rather to redefine human-AI collaboration. The goal is to leverage LLMs’ strengths in knowledge dissemination while preserving the empathy, contextual judgment, and ethical responsibility that remain central to high-quality patient care.

This review offers several methodological strengths that enhance the credibility and relevance of its findings. First, to our knowledge, it is the first systematic review focused specifically on LLMs in the context of preoperative and discharge education. Second, the review adopts a multidimensional evaluation framework that extends beyond outcome synthesis, providing a structured way to interrogate what is reported, what is missing and how unevenly different model attributes are represented. This approach yields a clearer and more systematic view of the evidence landscape than traditional narrative synthesis allows.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the overall number of eligible studies remains small, and a large proportion were pilot or early-stage implementations, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Second, due to the methodological heterogeneity in outcome measurement and evaluation criteria, all outcomes were descriptively summarized. Third, while the evaluation framework was applied systematically, the coding of sub-dimensions was constrained by the reporting quality of the original studies. Most included studies treated LLMs as black-box tools, lacking detailed description of model version, training updates, or prompt engineering — all of which may influence effectiveness. It is possible that some dimensions were underreported rather than truly absent. Finally, the evaluation framework by Abbasian et al.13, although comprehensive, was originally developed for interactive conversational models. In this review, it was applied to both interactive and non-interactive uses of LLMs, with empathy-related indicators coded as not applicable when no direct interaction occurred. This approach enabled consistent comparison across studies but may not capture nuances specific to non-interactive educational content.

Building on these limitations, several directions for future research warrant consideration. First, future research should aim to describe both the internal attributes of LLMs and how these attributes relate to psychological responses and behavior change. Greater standardization in outcome definitions and reporting will also be essential to support meta-analyses. Second, structured evaluation frameworks, such as the one used in this review13, should be applied more consistently to facilitate cross-study comparability and support clearer reporting across the full set of model characteristics, including dimensions such as trustworthiness and performance. As LLMs are increasingly used in both interactive and non-interactive ways, future frameworks may also need to distinguish these application modes more explicitly to capture their different implications for patient education. Third, the rapid pace of generative model development highlights the need for studies that report direct comparisons across LLM versions or architectures to clarify whether newer models lead to meaningful improvements in educational tasks. Fourth, greater attention should be paid to documenting outcomes among diverse patient populations, particularly individuals with limited health literacy or communication barriers, to assess the generalizability of LLM-based interventions across clinical contexts. Finally, longitudinal assessments are needed to determine whether any observed benefits of LLM-based education can be sustained in patient behavior, treatment adherence, and longer-term health outcomes beyond the immediate episode of care.

## Methods

### Review registration and research objectives

This systematic review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The protocol was registered in the PROSPERO database (Registration ID: CRD420251041371). The primary aim of this review was to evaluate the effectiveness of LLMs in delivering patient-centered preoperative and discharge education. A secondary aim was to apply a structured evaluation framework to examine the associations between different LLM characteristics and patient outcomes in delivering preoperative and discharge education.

### Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they (P) involved either actual surgical patients or simulated patient scenarios (commonly used in early-stage empirical evaluations where real-world deployment is not yet feasible) receiving preoperative education or discharge instructions; (I) directly utilized LLMs to generate, adapt, or deliver patient-centered educational content related to preoperative preparation or postoperative discharge; and (S) employed empirical research designs such as randomized controlled trials, quasi-experimental studies, cross-sectional studies, cohort studies, case-control studies, prospective or retrospective studies, or simulation-based evaluations. Studies were excluded if they (P) targeted caregivers, family members, or healthcare providers instead of patients; (I) used LLMs exclusively for internal documentation, administrative, or clinician-facing tasks without patient interaction; or (S) were conference abstracts, narrative reviews, editorials, letters, case reports, or lacked available full-text publications.

### Data sources and search strategy

Five databases (PubMed, Embase, CINAHL Plus, arXiv, and IEEE Xplore) were systematically searched from inception to April 18th, 2025. The full search strategies for each database are provided in Supplementary Table 2. The reference lists of all included studies were also screened to identify additional eligible records.

### Study selection and data extraction

All references retrieved from database searches were imported into EndNote 21 for deduplication. Following removal of duplicates, the remaining records were screened in two phases by two independent reviewers (MW and HM). The initial phase involved reviewing titles and abstracts against predefined eligibility criteria. Papers deemed potentially relevant proceeded to full-text screening, which was conducted independently and in duplicate. Discrepancies at either stage were resolved through discussion with the third reviewer (MP) to reach consensus.

A structured and pilot-tested data extraction form (Supplementary Table 3) was developed based on the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) data extraction framework and used to standardize information collection across studies. The finalized data extraction template is presented in Supplementary Table 4. All extracted data were cross-verified by the second reviewer to ensure completeness and consistency.

### Quality appraisal

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklists41, with tool selection based on study design. Specifically, the checklist for randomized controlled trials (13 items), quasi-experimental studies (9 items), or analytical cross-sectional studies (20 items) was applied as appropriate. Each item was rated as “Yes,” “No,” “Unclear,” or “Not Applicable” according to JBI guidance. Two reviewers (MW and HM) independently appraised each study in parallel. Any differences in scoring were reviewed collaboratively and reconciled following further evaluation of the original studies.

Prior to appraisal, we specified a set of critical items for each design. Studies rated “No” or “Unclear” on any critical item were not used in the effectiveness synthesis because their design or reporting did not support interpretation of intervention–outcome relationships. However, they were retained for the framework-based mapping, which aimed to document how LLM characteristics and patient outcomes were reported and where evidence gaps remained; this component does not require the methodological conditions needed for effectiveness interpretation.

For RCTs, critical items were chosen to ensure that between-group differences could be attributed to the intervention rather than allocation or procedural biases. These included true randomization (Q1) and allocation concealment (Q2), which prevent systematic differences at assignment, as well as comparable care aside from the intervention (Q6) and consistent outcome measurement between groups (Q8), which ensure that any observed differences can be interpreted in relation to the LLM-based educational material.

For cross-sectional studies, interpretability of effectiveness relies on whether the LLM-generated material constitutes a clearly defined exposure and whether patient-facing outcomes were assessed adequately. Accordingly, the critical items were valid and reliable measurement of the exposure (Q3) and valid and reliable measurement of outcomes (Q7).

For quasi-experimental designs, attribution of change to the intervention requires temporal contrast and comparable assessment across observation points. Critical items therefore included pre–post outcome measurement (Q5) and consistent outcome measurement across groups or time points (Q6).

### Evaluation framework and coding procedure

The structured evaluation framework developed by Abbasian et al.13 offers a systematic basis for assessing key characteristics of LLMs in the context of preoperative and discharge education. This framework was designed to assess the quality and comprehensiveness of healthcare conversations generated by LLMs. It consists of four primary dimensions: accuracy, trustworthiness, empathy, and performance. Each dimension contains multiple sub-dimensions. For instance, the accuracy dimension includes items such as intrinsic metrics, SSI, and robustness, while trustworthiness includes sub-dimensions such as bias, interpretability, and safety and security.

Although the framework was originally developed for interactive conversational models, three of its four primary dimensions—accuracy, trustworthiness, and performance—concern attributes of LLM outputs that are relevant regardless of whether the model interacts with patients directly or is used to generate educational material delivered by humans. For studies that did not involve interactive use, indicators under the empathy dimension were treated as not applicable, while the remaining dimensions were evaluated as specified in the original framework.

To apply the evaluation framework consistently, sub-dimensions were coded as present only when they were explicitly assessed using either quantitative indicators or qualitative evaluations. Unreported or non-applicable information was left blank in all summary tables. Coding decisions were independently made by two reviewers (MW and HM) based on information extracted from the methods and results sections of each study. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (MP). Detailed coding examples and rationale are provided in the full data extraction table (Supplementary Table 5).

### Data synthesis and statistical analysis

We synthesized all findings narratively rather than performing a meta-analysis because of substantial heterogeneity across study designs, populations, and LLM versions. Extracted data were organized by outcome domain, and within each domain we summarized study characteristics, intervention features, and primary findings.

To visualize reporting patterns, a heatmap was generated using Python (version 3.12.2) that displayed which specific sub-dimensions were reported in relation to outcome types. This descriptive mapping was intended to characterize current evaluation practices and highlight under-reported dimensions. No statistical comparison or causal inference was performed.

All data supporting the findings of this study are derived from the published literature included in the systematic review. No new datasets were generated or analyzed.
