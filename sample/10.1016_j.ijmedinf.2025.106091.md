ABSTRACT
Purpose: To synthesize performance and improvement strategies for adapting generative LLMs in EHR analyses 
and applications.
Methods: We followed the PRISMA guidelines to conduct a systematic review of articles from PubMed and Web of 
Science published between January 1, 2023 and November 9, 2024. Multiple reviewers including biomedical 
informaticians and a clinician involved in the article reviewing process. Studies were included if they used 
generative LLMs to analyze real-world EHR data and reported quantitative performance evaluations for an 
improvement technique. The review identified key clinical applications, summarized performance and the 
improvement strategies.
Results: Of the 18,735 articles retrieved, 196 met our criteria. 112 (57.1%) studies used generative LLMs for 
clinical decision support tasks, 40 (20.4%) studies involved documentation tasks, 39 (19.9%) studies involved 
information extraction tasks, 11 (5.6%) studies involved patient communication tasks, and 10 (5.1%) studies 
included summarization tasks. Among the 196 studies, most studies (88.8%) did not quantitatively evaluate the 
LLM performance improvement strategies, with the rest twenty-four studies (12.2%) quantitatively evaluated the 
effectiveness of in-context learning (9 studies), fine-tuning (12 studies), multimodal integration (8 studies), and 
ensemble learning (2 studies). Three studies highlighted that few-shot prompting, fine-tuning, and multimodal 
data integration might not improve performance, and another two studies found that fine-tuning a smaller model 
could outperform a large model.
Conclusion: Applying a performance improvement strategy may not necessarily lead to performance improve￾ment, and detailed guidelines regarding how to apply those strategies more effectively and safely are needed, 
which can be completed from more quantitative analysis in the future.

## 2. Methods
2.1. Data sources and searches
We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review 
and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and conducted systematic 
searches in PubMed and Web of Science (Fig. 1) [10]. The process 
involved several key steps: a Boolean search, removal of duplicates, 
screening of studies, and data extraction. The Boolean search was con￾ducted on November 09, 2024, using search terms and filters developed 
through team discussions. The search included LLM-related terms (e.g., 
“large language model”, “prompt engineering”) and the names of spe￾cific LLMs. The complete search query is provided in Supplementary 
Table S1.
2.2. Article reviewing
To focus on research articles presenting original data and quantita￾tive results, we excluded certain article types, such as reviews. Given 
that the initial release of ChatGPT was on November 30th, 2022, we 
included articles published from 2023 onward, with only peer-reviewed 
articles included, while preprints were excluded.
The selection process began with the removal of duplicate records, 
followed by a manual review of the deduplicated list. Eligibility criteria 
were clearly defined and further refined during the initial screening 
phase through weekly team meetings, ensuring consistent interpretation 
and mitigating potential ambiguity. Articles were excluded if they met 
one or more of the following criteria: (1) were of an ineligible type (e.g., 
preprints, reviews, editorials, comments); (2) did not involve generative 
LLMs—for instance, those describing chatbots without LLM integration; 
(3) focused solely on encoder-based models (e.g., Longformer [3], 
NYUTron [4], GatorTron) [5], despite their widespread use; (4) used 
LLMs exclusively for non-English communication; (5) applied LLMs in 
non-healthcare contexts (e.g., exam preparation, academic writing); (6) 
lacked quantitative evaluation (e.g., studies reporting only conversa￾tional transcripts with ChatGPT); (7) did not use original EHR data (e.g., 
relied on synthetic or aggregated datasets); or (8) failed to provide 
quantitative comparisons of LLM performance improvement strategies 
against a baseline model.
During the eligibility screening process, two reviewers initially 
screened a pilot set of 50 identical articles. If the inter-reviewer agree￾ment exceeded 90 %, they proceeded to independently screen the 
remaining articles. If the agreement rate was below 90 %, discrepancies 
were discussed, and an additional set of 50 articles was screened until 
the 90 % threshold was achieved. Articles that remained uncategorized 
due to uncertainty were discussed by the review team to reach a 
consensus classification.
For data extraction, we recorded the performance metrics reported in 
each study, the performance improvement strategies employed, and the 
clinical tasks addressed. Weekly meetings were held to discuss uncertain 
annotations and revise the data extraction strategy, ensuring clarity and 
consistency across reviewers. When available, we also documented re￾ported instances of common biases, errors, and hallucinations.
2.3. Risk of bias assessment.
We assessed the risk of bias in the review process using the ROBIS 
tool, which is increasingly applied in systematic reviews to evaluate 
review-level bias [11,12]. The assessment examined the extent to which 
our methods minimized bias, identified any residual risks, and recog￾nized limitations. ROBIS evaluation was conducted across four 
domains—study eligibility criteria; identification and selection of 
studies; data collection and study appraisal; and synthesis and fin￾dings—followed by an overall risk-of-bias judgment. Signaling questions 
were answered based on predefined eligibility criteria, detailed search 
and screening procedures, data extraction protocols, and synthesis 
methods.

## 3. Results
3.1. Studies employing generative LLMs in EHR
Out of 18,735 unique records, 196 studies applied generative LLMs 
to EHR since 2023 (Fig. 1). As illustrated in Fig. 2, the number of pub￾lications rose sharply from 26 in 2023 to 99 in the first half of 2024 
(2024H1). Based on the 71 articles published during the first four 
months of 2024H2 included in our dataset, we estimated approximately 
106 articles (71/4*6 ≈106) for the full half-year period 2024H2. 
ChatGPT was the most commonly used model in 2023 (47.5 %) but 
showed decreasing use over time (32.7 % in 2024H1, 21.6 % in 
2024H2), while GPT-4 usage increased (25.0 %, 29.8 %, and 34.2 %, 
respectively). The share of less frequently used models—grouped as 
“Others”—also grew over time, reaching 36.0 % in 2024H2. Llama-2 
and Gemini gained traction in 2024, each surpassing 5 % usage. Word 
cloud analysis revealed a gradual shift in thematic focus: while 
“ChatGPT,” “patient,” and “clinical” dominated early literature, terms 
such as “LLM”, “accuracy,” and “evaluation” became more prominent in 
2024.
In the included studies, LLMs were applied to a variety of clinical 
tasks, falling into the following major categories: 1) Clinical Decision 
Support (n = 112, 57.1 %); 2) Documentation (n = 40, 20.4 %); 3) In￾formation Extraction (n = 39, 19.9 %); 4) Patient Communications (n =
11, 5.6 %); 5) Summarization (n = 10, 5.1 %). Across all periods, clinical 
decision support remained the most common application of generative 
LLMs, although its relative share decreased over time—from 84.6 % in 
2023 to 52.3 % in 2024H1, and further to 48.8 % in 2024H2. Mean￾while, other task categories became more prominent. Documentation 
use cases increased steadily, rising from 7.7 % in 2023 to 19.6 % in 
2024H1 and 20.9 % in 2024H2. Notably, Information Extraction tasks 
grew from 7.7 % in 2023 to 15.9 % in 2024H1, and reached 25.6 % by 
2024H2—surpassing documentation in terms of relative share. Sum￾marization tasks first appeared in 2024H1 (6.5 %) and remained present 
in 2024H2 (less than 5.0 %), while conversational agents were consis￾tently underrepresented (5.6 % in 2024H1 and less than 5.0 % in 
2024H2).
As shown in Table 1, we summarize the performances, errors, and 
hallucinations for each clinical task category, highlighting a diverse 
range of performances and challenges associated with LLMs in analyzing 
EHRs for clinical tasks. 122 included studies used LLMs to perform 
clinical decision support-related tasks, 39 Studies used LLMs to perform 
information extraction related tasks, 11 studies that used generative 
LLMs to help with patient communication tasks, and 10 studies were 
about the summarization task.

3.2. Studies quantitatively assessed performance improvement strategies
Out of 196 studies that applied generative LLMs to EHR, we identi￾fied 24 of them (12.2 %) that employed a performance improvement 
strategy and compared with baseline methods quantitatively. Fig. 3
summarizes strategies included in these studies, which include in￾context learning, fine-tuning, multimodal data fusion, and ensemble. 
Among the 24 studies, 9 investigated in-context learning, 12 studied 
fine-tuning techniques, 8 tested multimodal data fusion methods, and 2 
analyzed the ensemble approach. Tables 2–5 summarizes the detailed 
findings. Notably, three studies reported potential performance degra￾dation led by applying a specific performance improvement strategy 
[13–15], and two studies mentioned that fine-tuning a small model 
might outperform a large model [16,17].

3.3. Risk of bias assessment
Detailed responses to each ROBIS signaling question are provided in 
Supplementary Table S2. In summary, there were no concerns regarding 
study eligibility criteria or data collection and study appraisal, as 
eligibility rules were prespecified, consistently applied, and supported 
by calibration and consensus procedures during screening and 
extraction.
Concerns in the identification and selection of studies stemmed from 
limiting searches to two databases (PubMed and Web of Science) and 
excluding preprints and other grey literature, as well as not using sup￾plementary search methods (e.g., citation chasing). Concerns in the 
synthesis and findings domain arose from the inability to conduct sta￾tistical pooling due to substantial heterogeneity in clinical tasks, model 
types, performance metrics, and evaluation methods. The review also 
could not assess robustness using funnel plots or sensitivity analyses

## 4. Discussion
Generative large language models (LLMs) have revolutionized 
natural-language processing, yet their application to real-world elec￾tronic health records (EHRs) is still in its infancy. In this systematic re￾view we catalogued how LLMs are being used across a spectrum of EHR￾driven clinical tasks and synthesized the main strategies employed to 
boost their performance. By charting this rapidly evolving landscape, 
our goal is to provide a roadmap for the safe, evidence-based develop￾ment and deployment of AI tools that can accommodate the complexity, 
heterogeneity, and workflow constraints characteristic of clinical 
practice.
Our results show that when applying performance improvement 
strategies to generative LLMs in the healthcare domain, several practical 
considerations must be taken into account. First, the choice of strategy 
often entails trade-offs between performance gains and resource de￾mands. For example, fine-tuning typically yields substantial accuracy 
improvements but may require extensive labeled clinical data, compu￾tational resources, and careful model versioning, particularly when 
applied to large-scale models [18,19]. Similarly, multimodal data fusion 
can significantly enhance model contextual understanding, yet it de￾pends on the availability and harmonization of multimodal data [20]. 
In-context learning, in contrast, offers a more lightweight and accessible 
method, especially when computational budgets are constrained, but its 
effectiveness may be sensitive to task complexity and model limitations 
[21]. Moreover, ensemble strategies, though potentially powerful in 
integrating complementary strengths of different model types, remain 
underexplored and require more research to understand their imple￾mentation feasibility in production environments. Developers and clin￾ical informatics teams must therefore carefully evaluate the cost-benefit 
profile of each method, consider data availability and institutional 
constraints, and plan for ongoing evaluation and updating of strategies 
as models evolve [22,23]. Additionally, we found that several studies 
lacked sufficient methodological detail, such as descriptions of fine￾tuning approaches and hardware specifications. To support respon￾sible performance optimization, it is essential to ensure transparency in 
reporting the rationale behind strategies, thereby promoting reproduc￾ibility and enabling others to build upon validated methods.

Several broader themes emerged from the reviewed studies that 
merit further consideration. First, the performance gap between pro￾prietary LLMs (e.g., GPT-4) and open-source models remains evident in 
some applications, particularly when prompt engineering is leveraged, 
yet open-source models offer greater customizability, transparency, and 
data privacy, making them preferable for institution-level deployments 
when properly fine-tuned. Second, some studies demonstrated perfor￾mance plateaus, where adding more in-context examples or extending 
fine-tuning epochs yielded diminishing returns [14], pointing to the 
need for efficient optimization strategies rather than brute-force scaling. 
Third, reproducibility remains a concern; studies varied in their docu￾mentation of method details (e.g., fine-tuning methods and used hard￾ware are missing in some studies), underscoring the importance of 
developing and adopting minimum reporting standards for LLM-based 
clinical studies. Additionally, given the evolving nature of clinical 
data, performance improvements achieved at one time point may 
degrade over time due to temporal drift, making continual learning and 
prompt revalidation essential components of any long-term deployment 
strategy [24]. Addressing these challenges will require ongoing inter￾disciplinary collaboration between clinicians, AI researchers, and 
informatics professionals to ensure that LLMs in healthcare are not only 
powerful but also trustworthy and sustainable.
To provide more actionable insights, we examined patterns within 
task-specific categories. For example, in-context learning was most 
frequently beneficial in decision support tasks requiring structured 
reasoning from examples, whereas fine-tuning tended to yield larger 
gains in complex summarization and phenotype normalization tasks 
that demand nuanced domain language understanding. Multimodal 
integration showed particular promise in scenarios combining imaging 
and text, such as radiology interpretation, but its benefits were less 
consistent in purely text-based contexts. Ensemble approaches, although 
less studied, demonstrated clear performance gains in tasks like early 
cognitive decline detection and emergency department admission pre￾diction by leveraging complementary strengths of LLMs and traditional 
machine learning models. These patterns can help researchers and 
practitioners align strategy selection with task requirements, available 
resources, and the clinical stakes of the application.
Moreover, the limited attention to bias in existing studies highlights 
a critical gap in the application of generative LLMs to EHR for clinical 
tasks. Although two studies reported minimal or no evidence of bias 
[25,26], some others revealed disparities in model performance based 
on patient demographics [27,28], underscoring the risk of perpetuating 
or amplifying health inequities through LLM-generated outputs. These 
inconsistencies suggest that LLMs may be sensitive to both the compo￾sition of their training data and the structure of input prompts, partic￾ularly when prompts contain examples that resemble certain 
subpopulations more closely than others. Given the potential for bias to 
impact clinical decision-making and patient safety, future work should 
prioritize comprehensive, systematic evaluations of LLM fairness across 
diverse patient groups and clinical contexts. Furthermore, transparency 
in model development and the use of bias mitigation strategies—such as 
balanced training datasets, adversarial testing, and fairness-aware 
prompting—are essential to ensure equitable and trustworthy LLM 
deployment in healthcare settings. As LLMs become increasingly inte￾grated into clinical workflows, addressing these ethical and methodo￾logical concerns will be critical for realizing their potential to support, 
rather than undermine, equitable patient care.
This review delivers a timely snapshot of the post-ChatGPT era by 
focusing exclusively on peer-reviewed studies that leverage real-world 
EHR data and by examining four major classes of performance￾enhancement strategies. Notably, we conducted a risk-of-bias assess￾ment to enhance transparency and ensure that readers are aware of 
potential sources of bias and the limitations underlying our findings and 
interpretations. Nevertheless, several limitations warrant attention. The 
field is evolving rapidly, and many reports are preliminary, raising the 
possibility of publication bias toward positive findings. Heterogeneity in 
task definitions, model architecture, and evaluation metrics precluded 
quantitative meta-analysis. As a result, the absence of a quantitative 
meta-analysis or pooled performance metrics limits the statistical gen￾eralizability of the findings. Moreover, limiting our search to peer￾reviewed literature from 2023 onward may have excluded valuable in￾sights from preprints or earlier work. In the future, researchers should 
prioritize external validation in diverse health-system contexts, more 
deeply explore ensemble and multimodal fusion methods, and improve 
reproducibility through transparent reporting and minimum-standard 
guidelines. Furthermore, standardization of LLM usage and evaluation 
methods is needed to enable meaningful statistical analyses across 
studies. Critically, technical advances must be paired with robust safe￾guards, including hallucination detection, bias mitigation, and fail-safe 
mechanisms, and with thoughtful integration into existing clinical 
workflows to ensure usability, interoperability, and regulatory compli￾ance. Meeting these challenges will be essential for the responsible, 
equitable, and durable adoption of generative LLMs in real clinical 
settings.

## 5. Conclusion
Our systematic review highlights the rapid evolution in the use of 
generative LLMs on real-world EHR data, marked by a transition from 
general-purpose models such as ChatGPT to more diverse and domain￾adapted alternatives. While early applications primarily targeted clin￾ical decision support, more recent studies have expanded to include 
tasks like information extraction and clinical documentation. A range of 
performance improvement strategies—such as in-context learning, fine￾tuning, multimodal integration, and model ensembling—have been 
employed. However, only a minority of studies quantitatively evaluated 
the effectiveness of these strategies, and just four studies examined po￾tential performance biases.
Importantly, the application of a performance improvement strategy 
does not guarantee improved outcomes. This underscores the need for 
systematic, quantitative evaluations to assess not only effectiveness but 
also fairness and safety in clinical settings. Future research should aim to 
establish evidence-based guidelines on when and how to apply specific 
strategies effectively. Such efforts will be critical to enabling the 
responsible, equitable, and impactful deployment of generative LLMs in 
healthcare.
