## ABSTRACT

Background: The rapid growth in research on ChatGPT’s healthcare applications has led to diverse evaluation methods and substantially heterogeneous findings, undermining evidence reliability and hindering clinical translation.  
Objectives: This review aims to examine how different evaluation methods shape our understanding of ChatGPT’s effectiveness in healthcare.  
Methods: Studies published between 2023 and 2024 that assess the use of ChatGPT in medical or healthcare￾related contexts were included. Evidence was obtained from peer-reviewed literature analyzing ChatGPT’s ap￾plications across clinical, educational, and diagnostic domains. Following the PRISMA guidelines, this systematic review analyzed 131 studies published during 2023–2024 that assess the use of ChatGPT in medical contexts.  
Results: The results indicate that predominant evaluation approaches—controlled trial studies, expert assessment studies, measurement-based evaluation studies, and prompt generation analysis studies—systematically influ￾ence conclusions about ChatGPT’s performance due to their inherent methodological characteristics, such as subjectivity, objectivity, and differences in ecological validity. Further analysis reveals that ChatGPT’s perfor￾mance is highly context-dependent, shaped by specific application scenarios, model versions, and prompting strategies.  
Conclusions: To address methodological heterogeneity and the lack of standardization, this study recommends multi-method cross-validation strategies and a risk-stratified, standardized evaluation framework. These steps are essential to enhance the scientific rigor and reliability of ChatGPT’s assessment in healthcare and to provide a solid foundation for its clinical integration.

## 1. Introduction

ChatGPT has garnered significant attention within the medical community for its impressive contextual understanding and coherent conversational abilities, which enable it to generate human-like re￾sponses [1]. It demonstrates immense potential in providing medical information, supporting clinical decision-making, enhancing medical education, facilitating telemedicine, and aiding medical research and data analysis [2–10]. Simultaneously, ChatGPT can be applied across multiple healthcare scenarios, including medical record keeping and translation, imaging interpretation, medication management, disease monitoring, clinical trial recruitment, and patient triage [11–18]. However, this potential is accompanied by rigorous scrutiny of its reliability, accuracy, and safety (Fig. 1).

As research evaluating ChatGPT’s medical applications has exploded, a major challenge has emerged: methodological heterogene￾ity. Different studies employing varying assessment methods yield starkly divergent results. Currently, researchers primarily use expert evaluations, controlled trials, quantitative measurements, and prompt optimization to assess ChatGPT’s medical performance. However, these approaches differ fundamentally in their evaluation dimensions, objec￾tivity, and reproducibility. The evaluation methods themselves have become a critical variable, directly leading to vastly divergent judg￾ments about ChatGPT’s performance and even contradictory conclu￾sions. ## 2.1. Methods

### 2.1.1. Literature search strategy

This study strictly adhered to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. A sys￾tematic literature search was conducted in two databases: PubMed and Web of Science Core Collection, covering publications from January 2023 to December 2024.

Database-specific search strings. ### 2.1.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

#### 2.1.2.1. Inclusion criteria. Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) original research articles published in peer￾reviewed journals; (2) primarily focused on evaluating ChatGPT or other GPT-series large language models (GPT-3.5, GPT-4, GPT-4o) in medical or healthcare settings; (3) involved clinical, diagnostic, educa￾tional, or patient care applications; (4) provided explicit evaluation methodologies with quantitative performance metrics (e.g., accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score); (5) published in English between January 2023 and December 2024; and (6) available as full-text articles (Table 1).

#### 2.1.2.2. Exclusion criteria. Studies were excluded if they: (1) were edi￾torials, commentaries, letters, or opinion pieces without original data; (2) were conference abstracts without full-text availability; (3) were duplicate publications or secondary analyses of the same dataset; (4) did not include performance evaluation or outcome measures; (5) lacked sufficient methodological details for quality assessment; (6) were non￾English publications; or (7) focused solely on technical model

### 2.1.3. Inter-rater reliability assessment

Two independent reviewers performed screening and data extraction at three critical stages. Cohen’s kappa (κ) was calculated to assess inter￾rater reliability, with disagreements resolved through consensus dis￾cussion or third-reviewer arbitration (Table 2).

#### 2.1.3.1. Title and abstract screening. A random sample of 200 records (10.5 %) was dual-screened, yielding substantial agreement (κ = 0.78, 95 % CI: 0.71–0.85). Disagreements in 18 records (9.0 %) were resolved through discussion.

#### 2.1.3.2. Full-text screening. Both reviewers independently assessed 80 full-text articles (25 %), demonstrating almost perfect agreement (κ = 0.85, 95 % CI: 0.76–0.94). Seven discrepancies (8.8 %) were resolved, with one requiring third-reviewer arbitration.

#### 2.1.3.3. Data extraction. Data from 40 studies (30 %) were indepen￾dently extracted, showing excellent agreement (κ = 0.88, 95 % CI: 0.81–0.95). Four disagreements (10 %) were resolved through re￾examination and consensus.

All kappa values exceeded the threshold of 0.60, indicating adequate reliability for systematic review processes. The high inter-rater agree￾ment across all stages supports the validity and reproducibility of our review findings.

### 2.1.4. Data extraction and coding

Two researchers independently screened the literature and extracted data, resolving discrepancies through mutual agreement. Extracted content included basic information (authors, year, country), study

development without healthcare application assessment. Deduplication was conducted using a hierarchical three-step approach with the following priority order:

1. Digital Object Identifier (DOI) matching: Records sharing identical DOIs were identified as duplicates. DOI-based deduplication was prioritized due to its high specificity and reliability as a unique identifier for scholarly publications  
2. PubMed Identifier (PMID) matching: For records lacking DOIs, PMID matching was employed as a secondary criterion to identify dupli￾cate entries from biomedical literature databases  
3. Title normalization and matching: Records without DOI or PMID were subjected to title-based deduplication. Titles were normalized through conversion to lowercase, removal of punctuation marks, and elimination of extra whitespace to ensure accurate matching despite minor formatting variations

This systematic deduplication procedure identified 328 duplicate records (14.68 % duplication rate), comprising 319 DOI-based dupli￾cates (97.3 %), 7 title-based duplicates (2.1 %), and 2 PMID-based du￾plicates (0.6 %). After removing duplicates, 1906 unique records remained for subsequent screening and analysis. The majority of du￾plicates were identified between the Web of Science exports and the summary dataset (n = 250, 76.2 %), while intra-source duplicates accounted for 76 records (23.2 %), primarily within the supplementary literature table (n = 70).

Based on content analysis, evaluation methods were categorized into four types: (1) Expert assessment: Medical experts subjectively evaluate ChatGPT performance. (2) Controlled trials: ChatGPT is compared against other methods using a control group. (3) Measurement-based evaluation: Objective quantitative metrics assess ChatGPT perfor￾mance. (4) Prompt generation analysis: ChatGPT performance optimi￾zation is evaluated through varying prompt designs.

The systematic review encompassed a total of 131 articles. The 131 studies have been categorized according to four primary research methodologies: controlled trial studies (n = 18), expert assessment studies (n = 52), measurement-based evaluation studies (n = 48), and prompt generation analysis studies (n = 13). For a more comprehensive overview of the extant literature on this subject, please refer to the reference list provided.

## 2.2. Research hot topics

### 2.2.1. Geographic, technological, and clinical application distribution

#### 2.2.1.1. Geographic distribution and global imbalance. Analysis of 1,624 studies revealed substantial geographic disparities in LLM research for medical applications. High-income countries (HICs) accounted for 1,104 studies (68.0 %), while low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) contributed only 362 studies (22.3 %), with 158 studies (9.7 %) having unknown affiliations. This distribution demonstrates a pronounced research concentration in economically developed regions (Table 3).

The United States dominated the research landscape with 524 studies (32.3 % of total), representing nearly one-third of global output. China ranked second with 220 studies (13.5 %), followed by Germany (n = 103, 6.3 %), Canada (n = 59, 3.6 %), and the United Kingdom (n = 59, 3.6 %). The top five countries collectively accounted for 1,015 studies (62.5 % of total), highlighting a marked geographic concentration.

Notably, the entire African continent, Latin America (excluding limited contributions from Brazil and Mexico), and Southeast Asia (excluding Singapore) were severely underrepresented, collectively contributing fewer than 50 studies (<3% of total). This geographic imbalance raises critical concerns regarding the generalizability of findings and equity in AI-driven healthcare innovation.

#### 2.2.1.2. Model version distribution. OpenAI’s GPT series dominated the technological landscape, accounting for 1,435 model instances (73.2 % of all specified models), reflecting both early market entry and wide￾spread accessibility. Google's models (Bard, Gemini, PaLM) represented 243 instances (12.4 %), while other models including Claude, LLaMA, and BERT comprised 282 instances (14.4 %).

Specific version analysis revealed that ChatGPT (unspecified version) was most frequently studied (n = 656, 28.5 %), followed by GPT-4 (n = 482, 21.0 %), and GPT-3.5 (n = 258, 11.2 %).

#### 2.2.1.3. Clinical application fields. Clinical decision support represented the most studied application domain (n = 525, 17.4 % of total appli￾cations), followed by surgery (n = 270, 8.9 %), medical education (n = 239, 7.9 %), and radiology/imaging (n = 221, 7.3 %). This distribution suggests prioritization of diagnostic and decision-making tasks over other clinical applications.

Certain medical specialties demonstrated disproportionate research attention. Ophthalmology (n = 161, 5.3 %) and urology (n = 107, 3.6 %) were relatively well-studied compared to primary care, nursing, and dermatology (all < 2 % each). This specialty-specific imbalance may reflect differences in data availability, commercial interest, or research infrastructure rather than clinical need (Table 4).

### 2.2.2. Key characteristics of included studies

Through systematic retrieval and screening, 131 eligible studies were included. The evidence shows a clear global imbalance, with the United States, China, and Germany contributing most ChatGPT-in￾medicine research. GPT-3.5 was used in 89 studies (52.7 %), GPT-4 in 58 (34.3 %), and 22 studies (13.0 %) compared multiple versions; existing reviews suggest GPT-4 achieves roughly 10–20 percentage points higher accuracy than GPT-3.5 on medical licensing exams.

Methodologically, expert assessment (52 studies) and measurement￾based approaches (48 studies) dominate, followed by controlled trials (18) and prompt-generation analyses (13), indicating that most work remains at an early performance-validation stage with few prospective or high–evidence-grade trials. Sample sizes were mainly medium (50–200 participants, 40 %), with 35 % large-scale (>200) and 25 % small-sample (<50) evaluations.

Study classification and assessment criteria.

1. Expert Assessment Studies  
Expert assessment studies were defined as investigations in which medical professionals (practicing physicians, specialists, medical edu￾cators) evaluate ChatGPT outputs using their domain expertise. Assessment standards include: (a) Accuracy judgment: comparing re￾sponses against established medical knowledge or clinical guidelines; (b) Clinical applicability: evaluating practical utility in real-world medical settings; (c) Safety assessment: identifying potential risks or harmful recommendations. Expert panels typically ranged from 3 to 20 reviewers, with inter-rater reliability reported using Cohen's kappa or intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) where available.

Systematic extraction of methodological details from the 52 expert assessment studies revealed pervasive under-reporting of inter-rater agreement metrics—a fundamental quality indicator for subjective evaluations. Reporting prevalence: Only 3 studies (5.8 %) explicitly reported quantitative inter-rater reliability statistics using established metrics:

Fleiss’ kappa: Draschl et al. (2023) employed Fleiss’ kappa to assess agreement among 3 orthopedic surgeons evaluating ChatGPT responses to complex musculoskeletal questions, demonstrating the utility of multi-rater agreement assessment in surgical specialties.

Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC): Lahat et al. (2023) used ICC to quantify consistency among gastroenterology experts rating ChatGPT’s ability to identify gastroenterological research questions, appropriate for continuous quality scores.

Cohen’s kappa: Paslı et al. (2024) reported Cohen’s kappa for two emergency physicians’ agreement on ChatGPT-4′s triage decision accu￾racy, suitable for binary or categorical judgments. One additional study (Al-Dujaili et al., 2023) mentioned conducting “inter-rater reliability analysis” for pharmacotherapy case evaluations but did not specify the metric employed or report numerical values.

The remaining 48 studies (92.3 %) either: (a) employed single￾evaluator designs without independent verification (n≈23, 44.2 %); (b) reported only narrative consensus processes such as “disagreements resolved through discussion” without quantifying initial agreement (n≈18, 34.6 %); or (c) made no mention of inter-rater reliability assessment whatsoever (n≈7, 13.5 %). Observed specialty patterns (caveat: extremely limited sample): Among the few studies reporting reliability metrics, all originated from specialties with either:

Established grading traditions: Orthopedics and gastroenterology, which have experience with inter-observer variability assessment in imaging interpretation and endoscopic findings, showed willingness to apply similar rigor to AI evaluation.

High-stakes decision contexts: Emergency medicine (triage de￾cisions), where reliability assessment is critical for patient safety.

Notably absent were reliability reports from studies in inherently subjective domains such as patient communication quality, empathy assessment, or treatment appropriateness judgment—precisely the areas where evaluator disagreement would be most expected and most important to document.

Study size effects: The limited data (n = 3) preclude formal analysis, but descriptively, studies reporting reliability metrics tended to be moderately sized (sample sizes 50–150 cases) rather than very small (<30) or very large (>200) studies.

Critical implication: The 94.2 % non-reporting rate represents a severe methodological transparency deficit in the expert assessment literature. Without documented evaluator consistency, the validity of these studies’ conclusions—particularly positive claims about ChatGPT’s “high clinical utility” or “excellent accuracy”—remains fundamentally uncertain. These findings may reflect individual evalu￾ator preferences, specialty-specific biases, or idiosyncratic judgment criteria rather than reproducible expert consensus. This gap is particu￾larly concerning given that expert assessment was the most common evaluation methodology (n = 52, 39.7 % of included studies), meaning that a substantial proportion of the evidence base lacks basic quality assurance metrics (Table 5).

3. Controlled Trial Studies

Controlled trials were characterized by systematic comparison of ChatGPT against predefined reference standards or comparators. Judg￾ment criteria employed: (a) Gold standard comparison: matching against validated reference answers or consensus diagnoses; (b) Head￾to-head physician comparison: blinded evaluation against human expert responses; (c) Guideline concordance: alignment with author￾itative clinical practice guidelines. These studies required objective, measurable outcome variables and appropriate statistical testing (McNemar’s test, paired t-tests, Fisher’s exact test) for within-group comparisons.

4. Measurement-based Evaluation Studies

Measurement-based evaluations utilized quantitative metrics as primary assessment criteria, including: (a) Natural language quality metrics (BLEU, ROUGE scores for text similarity); (b) Information retrieval metrics (accuracy, recall, F1-score, precision); (c) Read￾ability metrics (Flesch Reading Ease, Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level); (d) Quality assessment instruments (DISCERN, PEMAT scales). These studies provided objective, reproducible measurements with clear thresholds for acceptable performance.

5. Prompt Generation Analysis Studies

Prompt generation studies systematically investigated how varia￾tions in input prompt design affect ChatGPT performance. ### 2.2.3. Performance characteristics across application domains

The studies covered a broad range of application domains, including specialty areas such as neurosurgery, ophthalmology, urology, and cardiovascular medicine, as well as diagnostic assistance, patient consultation, medical education, and emergency medicine.

Methodological quality and risk of bias assessment. Methodological qual￾ity was assessed using adapted criteria from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Tools, tailored for AI evaluation studies. Assessment domains included: (1) Study design appropriateness: alignment between research question and methodology; (2) Sample size adequacy and representativeness; (3) Outcome measurement validity: use of validated instruments or objective metrics; (4) Blinding procedures: whether evaluators were blinded to response source (AI vs. human); (5) Statistical analysis rigor: appropriate tests and effect size reporting; (6) Reproducibility: adequate reporting of model version, prompts, and parameters.

Risk of bias was evaluated across key domains: (1) Selection bias: representativeness of clinical scenarios or patient cases; (2) Perfor￾mance bias: lack of standardization in prompts or evaluation proced￾ures; (3) Detection bias: subjective outcome assessment without validation; (4) Reporting bias: selective reporting of favorable out￾comes. Studies were classified as low, moderate, or high risk based on cumulative assessment across domains.

Model Version and Temporal Information Extraction: To address the impact of model updates on temporal validity and comparability, we systematically extracted model version information from included studies. Specific procedures included: (1) Extracting “model version/ release” information (e.g., “GPT-3.5-Turbo-0125”, “GPT-4–0613”) or model access date if version was unspecified; (2) Categorizing studies into standard versions (“GPT-3.5-Turbo”, “GPT-4”) when specific build numbers were mentioned; (3) Flagging studies as “unspecified” when version and date were absent; (4) Recording study execution timeframe for temporal analysis. These data enabled examination of: (a) perfor￾mance variations between studies using different versions of the same model at different time points; (b) longitudinal capability changes within model families (GPT-3.5 vs. GPT-4) as evidence of temporal validity and stability.

ChatGPT—particularly GPT-4—demonstrated superior performance in patient education, basic medical knowledge retrieval, and medical report simplification tasks, whereas human physicians retained clear advantages in complex clinical reasoning and nuanced patient communication.

### 2.2.5. Evaluation quality analysis

Among expert assessment studies, sample sizes varied significantly, ranging from single-expert evaluations to large-scale assessments involving 20 experts [25]. Consistency in evaluation criteria was notably lacking, with most studies employing Likert scales but lacking unified scoring systems [26,27]. Evaluation dimensions were diverse, encompassing accuracy, completeness, relevance, and comprehensi￾bility, often supplemented by readability tests [14,28].

Although some studies employed blinded assessments [29] and calculated inter- rater reliability (e.g., using Cohen’s kappa, Fleiss’ kappa, or intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC)), such quality control measures remain uncommon [30–32]. This results in a lack of direct comparability between expert ratings across studies, posing significant challenges for evidence synthesis.

Among studies on standardized examinations, medical licensing ex￾aminations were the most widely applied, followed by specialty board

exams, with the remainder using self-developed test banks. The strengths of these studies lie in their high objectivity and reproduc￾ibility, often employing rigorous statistical methods (e.g., Fisher’s exact test, post-hoc analyses of one-way ANOVA) to compare different models or performance differences relative to human examinees [24,33]. However, limitations include potential gaps from real clinical scenarios, and significant influence of exam difficulty, cultural context, and ques￾tion types on results, yielding pass rates ranging from low levels (46.8 %) 24 to exceptional levels [23]. Thus, performance assessed through exams may not directly extrapolate to real clinical practice value.

Comparative studies exhibit highly complex methodological char￾acteristics. Their design hinges on the selection of comparators, encompassing direct comparisons with human experts [34], traditional search engines [31,35], other AI models (e.g., Google Bard, Bing Chat) [24,30], and established guidelines [36]. Statistical analysis methods varied, including chi-square tests [31] or Fisher’s exact tests 31 for categorical variable comparisons, t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variable comparisons, and corre￾lation analyses such as Spearman’s correlation [37]. Notably, the lack of uniform standards for selecting control benchmarks led different studies addressing the same task category to draw entirely opposite conclusions, highlighting the decisive impact of methodological design on final outcomes.

Differences in prompt engineering and data analysis methods also constituted key drivers of research heterogeneity. Studies consistently noted the significant influence of prompt design on output quality [38], yet lacked unified prompting standards. Regarding data analysis, methodologies range from simple descriptive statistics to complex ma￾chine learning models (e.g., topic analysis using LDA [39]). Although many studies employ advanced statistical techniques (e.g., regression analysis, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis [20]), only a handful provide detailed reports on blinded implementation [40] or model parameter settings. This severely compromises the reproduc￾ibility of experiments and the reliability of results.

## 3. Discussion

## 3.1. Key findings

This systematic review elucidates three overarching findings that are critical for interpreting the burgeoning yet fragmented evidence on large language models (LLMs), exemplified by the ChatGPT series, in medicine.

First, and most critically, methodological heterogeneity is the

primary driver of contradictory conclusions. The choice of evaluation paradigm—be it expert appraisal, standardized testing, or comparative benchmarking—systematically and decisively shapes the reported per￾formance of LLMs. For instance, studies employing expert satisfaction ratings frequently report high utility, whereas those using rigorous, blinded comparative designs yield more variable and often less favor￾able outcomes. This fundamental discordance, rather than reflecting a true measure of capability, underscores a field-wide lack of standardized assessment, making direct comparisons across studies perilous and much of the current debate methodological in origin.

Second, LLM performance demonstrates pronounced context￾dependency, revealing both significant strengths and critical bound￾aries. Performance is highly contingent on task complexity and clinical risk level. Models excel in tasks of knowledge retrieval and application within structured, exam-like settings, at times matching or surpassing human performance on certain professional board examinations. How￾ever, their efficacy diminishes markedly in scenarios requiring higher￾order clinical reasoning, synthesis of ambiguous or multimodal infor￾mation, and nuanced patient communication. This stark contrast be￾tween proficiency in controlled assessments and the demands of authentic clinical workflows points to a substantial ecological validity gap.

Third, the current research landscape exhibits profound systemic imbalances that threaten the generalizability and equity of findings. A stark geographic concentration is evident, with the vast majority of studies originating from a handful of high-income countries, while vast regions of the globe remain severely underrepresented. This is com￾pounded by a parallel dominance of English-language evaluations. Consequently, the evidence base is built upon narrow cultural, linguis￾tic, and clinical contexts, raising serious concerns about the external validity of reported performance and the risk of perpetuating or exac￾erbating global health disparities through AI innovation.

Collectively, these findings argue that the central challenge in this field is not merely technological refinement but the urgent establish￾ment of a rigorous, transparent, and equitable evaluation science to reliably translate algorithmic potential into safe and effective clinical practice.

## 3.2. Methodological heterogeneity and implications for evidence credibility and clinical interpretation

The contradictory landscape of findings detailed in Section 3.1 can be systematically deconstructed by examining the inherent character￾istics, inherent biases, and divergent conclusions associated with each dominant evaluation paradigm.

### 3.2.1. Expert evaluation studies: the subjectivity-utility trade-off

In expert evaluation studies, ChatGPT consistently received high “satisfaction” or quality ratings in clinical contexts. For instance, experts positively assessed ChatGPT’s responses in pancreatic cancer care [25], rhinoplasty consultations [27], intraoperative decision support [26], liver transplantation [41], psychiatric case presentations [42], and or￾thodontics [43], praising its high information accessibility, content ac￾curacy, and value. Such studies often employ tools like Likert scales or Global Quality Scores (GQS), with multiple experts conducting inde￾pendent assessments to ensure reliability (e.g., using Fleiss’ kappa or Intraclass Correlation Coefficients ICC) [36]. The reported value lies in high information accessibility, accuracy, and clinical relevance.

However, this paradigm is intrinsically limited by subjectivity. Ex￾perts frequently highlight limitations, such as responses being overly general, failing to account for individual patient nuances [32], inability to identify all clinical factors [44], or lacking 100 % accuracy and completeness [45]. Beyond these documented limitations, deeper methodological concerns often go unreported: the potential for rater fatigue in evaluating large volumes of text, confirmation bias among evaluators who may be predisposed to either enthusiasm or skepticism about AI, and variability that extends beyond what kappa statistics capture. Consequently, while these studies highlight potential utility, their findings are difficult to standardize, compare, or generalize.

### 3.2.2. Standardized examinations: quantifiable but ecologically limited benchmarks

Standardized test results exhibit significant variability, with pass rates ranging from low levels such as GPT-3.5 scoring 46.8 % on a sleep medicine exam [24] to exceptional performance exemplified by GPT-4 outperforming human candidates on a neurosurgery exam [23]. A straightforward average pass rate is difficult to define, reflecting sub￾stantial impacts from exam difficulty, domain, and model version. This variance is heavily influenced by exam difficulty, specialty, and—most critically—model version, with GPT-4 consistently and markedly out￾performing GPT-3.5 [46]. Additionally, cognitive level disparities are evident, as ChatGPT excels at lower-order cognitive tasks involving recall and comprehension but faces greater challenges with higher-order tasks requiring application, analysis, and synthesis [47,48]. These studies employ objective metrics and statistical comparisons (e.g., Fisher’s exact test) against human benchmarks, offering high repro￾ducibility [23,24].

Research typically employs extensive multiple-choice question banks, using statistical methods like Fisher’s exact test to compare scores across models or against human examinees, often with sub-specialty analyses.

The primary critique of this approach is its compromised ecological validity. Standardized tests measure knowledge retrieval and applica￾tion in idealized, structured scenarios—a stark contrast to the ambig￾uous, multimodal, and iterative nature of real-world clinical reasoning [49]. Excelling at board-style questions does not equate to competency in generating differential diagnoses for complex, undifferentiated pa￾tients or integrating disparate clinical data.

### 3.2.3. Controlled comparative studies: context-dependent and comparator￾driven outcomes

Results from comparative studies show the greatest divergence, with conclusions heavily dependent on the choice of comparator. Findings may indicate ChatGPT outperforms physician responses 35 or under￾performs relative to traditional search engines (e.g., Google resource quality 4.2 vs. ChatGPT 3.6, p = 0.005) or other AI models [24,31]. Such studies feature complex designs, often employing blinded assessments and multiple statistical tests (e.g., t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, chi￾square tests, regression analysis) to rigorously control variables. They compare differences in accuracy, readability (Flesch Reading Ease Score), comprehensiveness, and other metrics across answer sources [50]. Results are highly susceptible to “manipulation” or conflicting interpretations.

This very rigor exposes the manipulability of the “AI vs. X” frame￾work. The outcome is not an absolute measure of capability but a rela￾tive one, heavily influenced by the strength of the comparator and the specific metrics (accuracy, readability, comprehensiveness) being emphasized. This leads to a fragmented evidence base where ChatGPT can be simultaneously framed as “superhuman” or “deficient” based on selective benchmarking.

### 3.2.4. The converging consequences: a crisis of reproducibility and interpretability

The heterogeneity described above—spanning subjective tools, un￾standardized prompts (a critical factor given ChatGPT’s sensitivity to input wording), unreported model version details, and the ecological gap—converges to create a fundamental crisis of reproducibility and evidence synthesis. Without a common methodological language and minimum reporting standards (e.g., for prompt engineering, model API version, evaluator blinding), individual studies remain isolated perfor￾mance demonstrations. This impedes the accumulation of a coherent, reliable evidence base necessary to inform clinical or policy decisions,

leaving the field in a state of perpetual controversy fueled by apples-to￾oranges comparisons.

## 3.3. Limitations and a roadmap for future evaluation

The insights from this review must be interpreted in light of its own limitations, which in turn illuminate a critical pathway for advancing the field toward more rigorous, equitable, and clinically meaningful assessment.

### 3.3.1. Limitations of the present review

First, this synthesis adopted a narrative rather than a quantitative meta-analytic approach. While this allowed for a nuanced exploration of methodological heterogeneity and contextual factors, it precluded the statistical pooling of effect sizes (e.g., aggregate accuracy rates) across studies, a step that could have provided more precise summary esti￾mates. Second, our assessment of certain biases, such as cultural and linguistic bias, was based on descriptive, narrative synthesis of geographic and language patterns rather than a formal, quantitative bias measurement tool applied to each study. Third, the quality and depth of the evidence we synthesized are inherently constrained by the variable methodological rigor of the included primary studies, particularly con￾cerning the inconsistent reporting of prompts, model versions, and evaluation protocols. Fourth, this review is constrained by its temporal scope (January 2023 to December 2024) and the inherent limitations of database searching. While this captures the peak publication period following ChatGPT's release, it excludes earlier foundational work and the most recent advancements. Additionally, despite a comprehensive search strategy, relevant studies published in non-indexed journals or in languages other than English may have been missed.

### 3.3.2. Synthesizing the evidence: performance boundaries, bias origins, and the translational gap

The methodological heterogeneity described in Section 3.2 directly manifests in the highly variable and context-dependent performance of ChatGPT, while also obscuring its true capability boundaries.

Scenario-Dependent Performance and Delineated Capability Boundaries. ChatGPT exhibits significant scenario dependency in its performance across different medical contexts, with its capability boundaries and applicability defined by task complexity and risk levels. It excels at information retrieval and reorganization based on vast training datasets but struggles with tasks requiring deep clinical reasoning and creative problem-solving. In medical education and knowledge assessment, it demonstrates robust knowledge reserves and reasoning abilities across multiple high-stakes certification exams￾essentially pattern matching and knowledge reproduction. For instance, it has passed China’s national physician, pharmacist, and nurse licensing exams [51], achieved outstanding results in the American Board of Sleep Medicine exam and the Board of Neurosurgery exam [23,24], even matching or exceeding the average human candidate’s performance. However, it failed the Polish Internal Medicine Certifica￾tion Exam [52]. In orthopedic specialty exams, its accuracy rate was lower than that of orthopedic residents [20]. This indicates limitations in exams requiring deep specialized reasoning or specific formats (e.g., complex multiple-choice questions). Beyond exam preparation, ChatGPT demonstrates potential in medical education, such as gener￾ating exam questions [38], simplifying radiology reports for patient comprehension, and providing psychodynamic formulas for teaching reference. However, its generated content requires rigorous expert re￾view to prevent errors.

In clinical decision support, it shows strong application potential for generating differential diagnosis lists. Its generated differential di￾agnoses have been validated by internal medicine specialists for accu￾racy in complex clinical scenarios, and its early differential diagnosis generation in emergency departments performed comparably to retro￾spective expert reviews [20]. It excels in triage decisions and evaluating neurological examinations using established scales (e.g., GCS, ICH), demonstrating potential as an auxiliary tool [53]. However, its perfor￾mance in mass casualty triage may be inferior to other AI models (e.g., Google Bard). It also exhibits instability and potential risks when providing specific treatment recommendations, such as overlooking critical clinical context in urological health advice [54] or offering postoperative guidelines with insufficient operational feasibility that cannot replace clinicians’ comprehensive judgment.

Regarding patient communication and education, ChatGPT can organize information in a structured, understandable manner and has been evaluated as a valuable resource for dispelling misinformation about stem cell therapy and answering questions about cardiac arrest. However, its generated answers are typically lengthy, highly academic, and poorly readable, requiring a college-level reading proficiency to comprehend [55], limiting its applicability to the general public. Furthermore, its performance may decline in non-English contexts, and the readability of its responses can sometimes be poor. This variability necessitates careful evaluation based on specific scenarios in practical applications.

Root Causes of Methodological Bias and the Reproducibility Crisis. The methodological disparities revealed in this review stem from a widespread lack of standardization, introducing multiple risks of bias. This poses multiple challenges to research quality, severely undermining the reliability and reproducibility of findings. Assessment tools and standards are subjective and heterogeneous. While expert evaluation is widely employed, variations in sample sizes (ranging from a few to twenty experts 25), assessment scales, and methodologies across studies introduce subjectivity that compromises objectivity [29]. Quantitative metrics offer greater objectivity, yet the assessment tools employed remain inconsistent. Researchers operate independently, preventing result aggregation. For instance, readability assessments use either the Flesch Reading Ease Score or the Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level [55]; quality evaluations employ either DISCERN or PEMAT. Variations in answer quality definitions and operationalization methods make direct result comparisons difficult.

A notable limitation in contemporary research pertains to the absence of standardization in prompt engineering. It is evident that subtle variations in prompt wording can result in substantial alterations to the outputs generated by ChatGPT, as the responses of this model are found to be highly sensitive to the particulars of the input prompt, including its wording, complexity, and context. A substantial degree of variability is evident across studies in the formulation of questions, the level of detail, and the utilization of few-shot learning, a factor that directly contributes to considerable fluctuations in performance. However, the majority of studies do not report or standardize their prompt strategies [38]. This inconsistent prompting approach engenders reproducibility challenges, impeding the ability of other researchers to fully replicate experimental procedures and validate findings.

Confusion effects from model versions and iterations: Different studies employ varying model versions (e.g., GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Bard), yet performance gaps exist between generations (e.g., GPT-4 generally outperforms GPT-3.5 by a wide margin) [24]. Many studies fail to explicitly specify the exact model version or access time, and large language models undergo continuous updates. This results in highly time-sensitive outcomes, short-lived conclusions, and poor comparability.

Finally, a discrepancy exists between evaluation scenarios and real-world settings. Many standardized tests or controlled studies employ idealized scenarios, such as answering isolated exam questions or spe￾cific queries [52]. This contrasts sharply with clinical applications requiring multimodal information integration (imaging, lab data), continuous multi-turn conversations, and interpretation of ambiguous chief complaints. Collectively, these factors constitute the root causes of the “reproducibility crisis” described in Section 3.2.4, reducing indi￾vidual studies to isolated “performance demonstrations” and impeding the accumulation of coherent evidence capable of guiding clinical

practice.

Collectively, these issues indicate that the field faces a “reproduc￾ibility crisis.” Without establishing a common methodological language and minimum reporting standards, research on ChatGPT’s medical ap￾plications will struggle to progress from fragmented “performance demonstrations” to accumulating evidence capable of guiding clinical practice.

### 3.3.3. Toward minimum reporting standards and a risk-stratified validation framework

To address the reproducibility crisis, the field must urgently adopt mandatory minimum reporting standards. Future studies should be required to transparently document: (1) the specific model version and access date (e.g., gpt-4–0613, accessed July 2023); (2) the complete prompt(s) used, including any few-shot examples or role-setting in￾structions; (3) the background of human evaluators and the blinding procedures employed; and (4) a clear, operational definition of all pri￾mary outcome metrics.

Beyond reporting, evaluation design must evolve. We propose a risk￾stratified, multi-method validation framework. For high-risk applica￾tions (e.g., diagnostic and treatment recommendations), evaluation should integrate three pillars: technical validation (objective metrics on benchmark tasks), clinical validation (multi-expert blinded reviews [25,26] against gold standards or real clinician performance, as employed in rigorous studies [31]), and implementation validation (usability and safety testing in simulated or real workflows). For low-risk applications (e.g., information retrieval), prioritize answer accuracy and comprehensibility 56. A comprehensive framework should encompass technical validation (using standardized tests) [23,24], clinical valida￾tion (combining expert assessment and real-world comparisons) [25,34], and implementation validation (focusing on workflow inte￾gration and ethical considerations).For instance, a prospective study on LLM-assisted triage for chest pain could first measure diagnostic accu￾racy on a curated case bank, then have emergency physicians blindly compare its recommendations to standard protocols, and finally assess its integration speed and error modes within a clinical dashboard.

### 3.3.4. Prioritizing equity and ecological validity in future research

Finally, the field must consciously correct the systemic imbalances identified in this review. Future research has an ethical and scientific imperative to:

Actively incorporate multicultural and multi-language test sets to assess performance across diverse patient populations and healthcare settings.  
Conduct rigorous localized validation in low-resource environments where clinical needs, disease burdens, and infrastructural constraints differ vastly from high-income settings.  
Re-conceptualize “efficacy” to include contextual suitability, moving beyond the question of “Is the model accurate?” to “For whom, and under what specific conditions, is it accurate and beneficial?”

Given LLM technology’s rapid evolution, evaluation criteria cannot remain static. Continuous benchmarking and reassessment mechanisms must be established to ensure timely and relevant outcomes as standards evolve with technological advancements. The path forward requires a deliberate shift from fragmented performance demonstrations to the conscientious practice of evaluation science. By enforcing transparency, adopting context-sensitive and risk-stratified frameworks, and centering equity, the research community can ensure that the development and deployment of LLMs in medicine are guided by robust, reproducible, and just evidence.

## 4. Conclusions

This systematic review demonstrates that the current evidence regarding the performance of large language models (LLMs) like ChatGPT in medicine is fundamentally shaped—and fragmented—by methodological heterogeneity. The choice of evaluation paradigm (expert appraisal, standardized testing, or comparative benchmarking) is not a neutral technical detail but a decisive variable that systemati￾cally influences conclusions, explaining much of the apparent contra￾diction in the literature.

Beyond methodological discord, our analysis reveals two further critical constraints: pronounced context-dependency of LLM perfor￾mance, with a significant gap between proficiency in structured exams and capability in complex clinical reasoning, and profound systemic imbalances in the research landscape, where geographic and linguistic concentration threatens the generalizability and equity of findings.

These findings hold significant implications for both research and clinical translation of ChatGPT in healthcare. Researchers must fully consider the impact of evaluation methods on outcomes when designing and interpreting ChatGPT assessment studies, while clinicians and pol￾icymakers need to synthesize results from diverse evaluation ap￾proaches. Most critically, this review underscores the acute lack of methodological standards in the field. Therefore, the primary challenge is no longer merely to document model capabilities but to establish a rigorous, transparent, and equitable evaluation science. This necessi￾tates an immediate shift toward adopting mandatory minimum report￾ing standards, implementing risk-stratified, multi-method validation frameworks, and consciously designing studies for ecological validity and global relevance. Only through such a concerted effort can the field progress from producing fragmented, often non-reproducible perfor￾mance demonstrations to generating a coherent, reliable evidence base capable of guiding the safe, effective, and just integration of LLMs into healthcare.
